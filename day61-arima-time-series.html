MachineLearningFromZero · Day 61

📈 ARIMA — the first model in this whole series where the rows are not interchangeable. Every estimator so far, from linear regression (D1) to one-class SVM (D58), could have had its rows shuffled without changing a single number, because they all assumed the observations were independent draws. A time series destroys that assumption on purpose: the value today is made of the value yesterday, the order is the signal, and a random train/test split leaks the future into the past. Day 59's HMM had hidden states and Day 60's survival analysis had time-to-event, but neither one forecasts — this is the first page in the series that says what happens next. The whole edifice rests on one precondition, stationarity: the mean, the variance and the autocorrelations must not depend on when you look, because a model whose parameters are estimated from the past can only be carried into the future if the rules did not change. Real series are almost never stationary, which is what the I is for — differencing ∇y_t = y_t − y_{t−1}, repeated d times, kills a deterministic trend and tames a random walk, and every forecast then has to be integrated back up to get to the scale anyone cares about. Once the series is flat, two exact diagnostics tell you the shape of what is left: the ACF ρ_k = γ_k/γ_0 with γ_k = (1/n)Σ(y_t−ȳ)(y_{t+k}−ȳ), and the PACF — the correlation at lag k with every shorter lag already removed — computed here by the Durbin–Levinson recursion, both drawn against the ±1.96/√n band that says which spikes are real. Read them together and you get the classic table: AR(p) → PACF cuts off at p, MA(q) → ACF cuts off at q. Then the fitting, done two independent ways so they can be checked against each other: Yule–Walker solves a Toeplitz system of autocorrelations with a hand-written Gaussian elimination, and ordinary least squares stacks lagged design rows and solves its own normal equations — they agree closely and disagree in a way that is itself informative. The MA part is genuinely harder, because its regressors are the errors and you have never seen an error, so this page implements Hannan–Rissanen for real: fit a long AR to manufacture residuals, then regress on lagged y and those lagged residuals. Forecasting is a recursion that feeds its own output back in — which is why an AR(1) with |φ|<1 decays back to the mean and why the honest forecast of a random walk is a flat line — and the uncertainty comes from the ψ-weights of the MA(∞) form with Var(e_h) = σ²Σψ_j², a band that must widen. Finally the two things that stop it being astrology: AIC/BIC over a live (p,d,q) grid, and a rolling-origin backtest against the naive and seasonal-naive baselines — because a forecast that cannot beat "tomorrow equals today" is worthless no matter how good the equations look. (Everything on this page is computed live in your browser from real numbers — no libraries, no canned results. A Node suite extracts the exact maths block shipped in this file and checks it against independent brute-force baselines and closed forms, passing NASSERT assertions.)

The order of the rows is the entire dataset. ARIMA is three ideas bolted together: AR — today is a weighted sum of recent days; I — difference the series until it stops drifting, then integrate the forecast back up; MA — today also carries the echo of recent surprises. The ACF and PACF tell you which of those you need, Yule–Walker and OLS fit the AR part two independent ways, Hannan–Rissanen recovers the unobservable MA errors, the ψ-weights turn a point forecast into a widening interval, AIC/BIC picks the order, and a rolling-origin backtest against "tomorrow equals today" decides whether any of it was worth doing. All of it is implemented from scratch below and runs live.

🔨 Build it yourself

    
ARIMA is a correlogram, a Toeplitz solve, a two-stage regression and one recursion — about 200 lines of arithmetic with no dependencies. Below is the exact runnable core the LOOK tab uses: the autocovariance, the Durbin–Levinson PACF, a Gaussian-elimination solver, Yule–Walker and OLS side by side, Hannan–Rissanen for the MA part, differencing and integration, the forecast recursion, the ψ-weights, AIC/BIC, and a rolling-origin backtest.

    
    

      
  1. 
        
    1
    The precondition — stationarity, and a test for it
    
        
    ARIMA estimates a fixed set of numbers from the past and then carries them into the future, which is only defensible if the rules did not change: constant mean, constant variance, and autocovariances that depend on the gap between two points and not on when they happened. That is weak stationarity, and it is the whole precondition. The augmented Dickey–Fuller test regresses Δy_t on y_{t−1} plus a few lagged differences and asks whether the level has any pull at all; ρ = 0 means a unit root, so a statistic near zero is bad news and a strongly negative one is what you want. Note the direction carefully — the null hypothesis is non-stationarity, so failing to reject is the default, not a result.
    
        
    // Delta y_t = alpha + rho*y_{t-1} + sum gamma_i * Delta y_{t-i} + e_t
function adfTest(y, lags) {
  const n = y.length;
  if (lags === undefined) lags = Math.max(1, Math.floor(Math.pow(n - 1, 1/3)));
  const dy = diff1(y), rows = [], targ = [];
  for (let t = lags; t < dy.length; t++) {
    const row = [1, y[t]];                    // dy[t] = y[t+1]-y[t], so y[t] IS the lagged level
    for (let i = 1; i <= lags; i++) row.push(dy[t - i]);
    rows.push(row); targ.push(dy[t]);
  }
  const f = olsFit(rows, targ);               // se from s^2 * (X'X)^-1, computed here
  return { stat: f.beta[1] / f.se[1], rho: f.beta[1], se: f.se[1], lags };
}
// MacKinnon critical values (constant, no trend): 1% -3.46  5% -2.88  10% -2.57
// stat > -2.88  =>  cannot reject a unit root  =>  DIFFERENCE IT FIRST.
// The null is NON-stationarity. "Not rejected" is not evidence of stationarity.

    The "I" — differencing, and integrating the forecast back up
    
        
    Differencing is the entire integrated part, and it is one subtraction: ∇y_t = y_t − y_{t−1}. Doing it once removes a linear trend and turns a random walk into white noise; twice removes a quadratic trend. Every difference costs you one observation and, far more importantly, changes the units of everything downstream — so the model is fitted on differences but nobody wants a forecast of differences, which means you must be able to walk it back up by cumulative summation from the last observed levels. That round trip is exactly invertible, which makes it one of the best unit tests in the whole pipeline: difference a series d times, integrate it back, and you must recover the original to machine precision.
    
        
    const diff1 = y => { const o = []; for (let t = 1; t < y.length; t++) o.push(y[t] - y[t-1]); return o; };

function differenceKeep(y, d) {              // keep what you need to undo it
  const inits = []; let z = y.slice();
  for (let i = 0; i < d; i++) { inits.push(z[0]); z = diff1(z); }
  return { z, inits };                       // z.length === y.length - d
}
function integrate(z, inits) {               // the exact inverse
  let cur = z.slice();
  for (let i = inits.length - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
    let acc = inits[i]; const out = [acc];
    for (const v of cur) { acc += v; out.push(acc); }
    cur = out;
  }
  return cur;
}
// to push a FORECAST back up you cumulate from the LAST level, not the first:
function lastLevels(y, d) { const L = []; let z = y.slice();
  for (let i = 0; i < d; i++) { L.push(z[z.length-1]); z = diff1(z); } return L; }
function integrateForecast(L, zf) {
  let cur = zf.slice();
  for (let i = L.length - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
    let acc = L[i]; const out = [];
    for (const v of cur) { acc += v; out.push(acc); }
    cur = out;
  }
  return cur;
}
// TEST: integrate(differenceKeep(y,d).z, inits) === y, to 1e-9, for d = 1,2,3.

    3
    The ACF — autocovariance, and which spikes are real
    
        
    The autocorrelation function is the correlation of a series with itself shifted by k. Compute the sample autocovariance γ_k = (1/n)Σ(y_t−ȳ)(y_{t+k}−ȳ) — note the divisor is n, not n−k, even though only n−k products are summed, because that choice guarantees the resulting covariance matrix is positive semi-definite and every downstream solve stays sane. Then ρ_k = γ_k/γ_0, so ρ_0 = 1 by construction. Under white noise each ρ̂_k is approximately N(0, 1/n), which gives the ±1.96/√n band — and about one lag in twenty will poke outside it by pure chance, so a single stray spike at lag 13 is not a discovery.
    
        
    function acovf(y, K) {
  const n = y.length, m = mean(y), g = new Array(K + 1).fill(0);
  for (let k = 0; k <= K; k++) {
    let s = 0;
    for (let t = 0; t + k < n; t++) s += (y[t] - m) * (y[t + k] - m);
    g[k] = s / n;                     // divide by n, NOT by (n-k): keeps the matrix PSD
  }
  return g;
}
const acf      = (y, K) => { const g = acovf(y, K); return g.map(v => v / g[0]); };
const acfBand  = n => 1.959963984540054 / Math.sqrt(n);

// rho_0 === 1 always. |rho_k| <= 1 always. Both are free assertions.
// A slowly, almost LINEARLY decaying ACF that stays high for 20+ lags is the
// fingerprint of a unit root -- difference the series and look again.

    The PACF — Durbin–Levinson, the direct effect only
    
        
    In an AR(1) with φ = 0.8, lag 2 has an autocorrelation of 0.64 — but none of it is direct, it is entirely the lag-1 effect applied twice. The partial autocorrelation at lag k strips that out: it is the correlation between y_t and y_{t−k} with lags 1…k−1 already regressed away, which is exactly the last coefficient φ_{kk} of a fitted AR(k). You could get it by solving a fresh k×k Yule–Walker system for every k, at cost O(K⁴); the Durbin–Levinson recursion gets the identical answer in O(K²) by updating the previous solution. The verification suite runs both and asserts they agree to twelve decimals, which is the cheapest way to be sure the recursion's index arithmetic is right.
    
        
    function pacfDL(y, K) {
  const r = acf(y, K), pac = new Array(K + 1).fill(0); pac[0] = 1;
  let prev = [];                                   // phi_{k-1, 1..k-1}
  for (let k = 1; k <= K; k++) {
    let num = r[k], den = 1;
    for (let j = 1; j < k; j++) { num -= prev[j-1] * r[k-j]; den -= prev[j-1] * r[j]; }
    const pkk = num / den;                         // <-- the partial autocorrelation
    const cur = new Array(k); cur[k-1] = pkk;
    for (let j = 1; j < k; j++) cur[j-1] = prev[j-1] - pkk * prev[k-j-1];
    pac[k] = pkk; prev = cur;
  }
  return pac;
}
// pacf[1] === acf[1] exactly.
// pacf[k] === (last coefficient of the k x k Yule-Walker solve) to 1e-12.
// Same band as the ACF: +/- 1.96/sqrt(n).

    Fitting AR(p) twice — Yule–Walker and OLS
    
        
    Multiply the AR equation by y_{t−k} and take expectations and the Yule–Walker equations fall out: ρ_k = Σφ_iρ_{k−i} for k = 1…p, a p×p Toeplitz system in the sample autocorrelations. Solve it with your own Gaussian elimination and you have the coefficients without ever building a design matrix. The completely different route is to treat it as a regression — stack rows [1, y_{t−1}, …, y_{t−p}] and solve the normal equations XᵀXβ = Xᵀy. The two answers converge as n grows but differ in finite samples for an honest reason: Yule–Walker uses all n observations through the autocovariance and is guaranteed to return a stationary model, while OLS conditions on the first p points, throws them away, and can happily hand you an explosive root.
    
        
    function solveLin(Ain, bin) {                  // Gaussian elimination, partial pivoting
  const n = bin.length, A = Ain.map(r => r.slice()), b = bin.slice();
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    let piv = i;
    for (let r = i+1; r < n; r++) if (Math.abs(A[r][i]) > Math.abs(A[piv][i])) piv = r;
    if (Math.abs(A[piv][i]) < 1e-13) return null;          // singular
    if (piv !== i) { [A[i],A[piv]] = [A[piv],A[i]]; [b[i],b[piv]] = [b[piv],b[i]]; }
    for (let r = i+1; r < n; r++) {
      const f = A[r][i] / A[i][i];
      for (let c = i; c < n; c++) A[r][c] -= f * A[i][c];
      b[r] -= f * b[i];
    }
  }
  const x = new Array(n).fill(0);
  for (let i = n-1; i >= 0; i--) { let s = b[i];
    for (let c = i+1; c < n; c++) s -= A[i][c] * x[c]; x[i] = s / A[i][i]; }
  return x;                                     // TEST: max|A*x - b| < 1e-10
}

function yuleWalker(y, p) {                     // route 1: the Toeplitz system
  const g = acovf(y, p), r = g.map(v => v / g[0]), A = [], b = [];
  for (let i = 1; i <= p; i++) {
    const row = []; for (let j = 1; j <= p; j++) row.push(r[Math.abs(i - j)]);
    A.push(row); b.push(r[i]);
  }
  const phi = solveLin(A, b);
  let s = 0; for (let i = 1; i <= p; i++) s += phi[i-1] * r[i];
  return { phi, sigma2: g[0] * (1 - s), mu: mean(y) };
}

function arOLS(y, p) {                          // route 2: lagged design rows
  const rows = [], targ = [];
  for (let t = p; t < y.length; t++) {
    const row = [1]; for (let i = 1; i <= p; i++) row.push(y[t - i]);
    rows.push(row); targ.push(y[t]);
  }
  const f = olsFit(rows, targ);                 // solves X'X beta = X'y
  return { c: f.beta[0], phi: f.beta.slice(1), sigma2: f.sse / f.nObs, fit: f };
}
// p = 1 has a closed form both routes must match: phi_hat = gamma_1 / gamma_0.

    MA(q) — regressors you have never seen, and Hannan–Rissanen
    
        
    An MA(q) says y_t = c + e_t + θ₁e_{t−1} + … + θ_qe_{t−q}, and there the pleasant regression story ends, because the regressors are the innovations and innovations are not observed — you cannot build a design matrix out of a column you do not have. Full maximum likelihood solves it with a Kalman filter and a nonlinear optimiser; Hannan–Rissanen solves it with two ordinary regressions, which is why it fits on this page. Stage one fits a deliberately over-long AR and keeps its residuals as a stand-in for the innovations; stage two regresses y_t on lagged y and those manufactured residuals. It is consistent, it needs no optimiser and no starting values, and it is what you use to seed a proper likelihood fit.
    
        
    function hannanRissanen(z, p, q, mLong) {
  const n = z.length;
  if (q === 0) { const a = arOLS(z, p); return { c: a.c, phi: a.phi, theta: [] }; }
  if (!mLong) mLong = Math.max(p + q + 2,
                     Math.min(Math.round(Math.sqrt(n)) + 2, Math.floor(n / 4)));

  // STAGE 1 — a long AR whose residuals stand in for the unobservable shocks
  const st1 = arOLS(z, mLong), eh = new Array(n).fill(0);
  for (let t = mLong; t < n; t++) eh[t] = st1.fit.resid[t - mLong];

  // STAGE 2 — now the MA columns EXIST, so it is just another regression
  const start = mLong + Math.max(p, q), rows = [], targ = [];
  for (let t = start; t < n; t++) {
    const row = [1];
    for (let i = 1; i <= p; i++) row.push(z[t - i]);       // AR columns
    for (let j = 1; j <= q; j++) row.push(eh[t - j]);      // MA columns (estimated!)
    rows.push(row); targ.push(z[t]);
  }
  const f = olsFit(rows, targ);
  return { c: f.beta[0], phi: f.beta.slice(1, 1+p), theta: f.beta.slice(1+p), mLong };
}
// The cost of stage 1 is real: you burn mLong observations before fitting anything.
// Recover the model's own residuals afterwards by running the recursion forward:
//   e_t = z_t - c - sum phi_i z_{t-i} - sum theta_j e_{t-j},  e = 0 before the start.

    Forecasting — a recursion that eats its own output
    
        
    One step ahead is just the fitted equation evaluated at the end of the data. Two steps ahead you do not have y_{n+1}, so you substitute the forecast you just made — and future shocks are replaced by their expectation, which is zero. That single substitution explains the two most-quoted facts in forecasting. An AR(1) with |φ| < 1 gives ŷ_{n+h} = μ + φ^h(y_n − μ), so the forecast decays geometrically back to the mean and long horizons are just the mean. A random walk has φ = 1, nothing decays, and the optimal forecast is a perfectly flat line at the last observed value — which is not a failure of the method, it is the correct answer, and it is exactly why the naive baseline is so hard to beat on financial data.
    
        
    function forecastARMA(z, m, h) {
  const p = m.phi.length, q = m.theta.length, n = z.length;
  const e = armaResiduals(z, m).e.slice();     // in-sample shocks; future ones are 0
  const zz = z.slice(), out = [];
  for (let s = 0; s < h; s++) {
    let v = m.c;
    for (let i = 1; i <= p; i++) v += m.phi[i-1] * zz[n + s - i];   // may be a FORECAST
    for (let j = 1; j <= q; j++) { const idx = n + s - j;
      v += m.theta[j-1] * (idx < n ? e[idx] : 0); }                 // E[future shock] = 0
    zz.push(v); e.push(0); out.push(v);
  }
  return out;
}
// then push it back to the original scale:
//   yf = d > 0 ? integrateForecast(lastLevels(y, d), zf) : zf

// AR(1) closed form the test checks against:  mu + phi^h * (y_n - mu),  mu = c/(1-phi)
// ARIMA(0,1,0):  every differenced forecast is 0  =>  the level forecast is FLAT.
// The MA(q) part is dead after q steps -- beyond that only the AR part is talking.

    ψ-weights — why the interval has to widen
    
        
    Every stationary ARMA can be rewritten as an infinite moving average, y_t = μ + Σψ_je_{t−j}, and the ψ-weights are just the impulse response: how much of a shock today is still visible j steps later. Get them from the recursion ψ_j = θ_j + Σφ_iψ_{j−i} with ψ_0 = 1. The h-step forecast error is then the sum of the shocks you have not seen yet, so Var(e_h) = σ²Σ_{j<h}ψ_j² — a strictly increasing quantity, which is why any honest forecast band widens. For a stationary AR(1) the ψ-weights are φ^j and the sum converges to σ²/(1−φ²): the band flattens out at the unconditional variance. For a random walk every ψ_j = 1 and the variance is σ²h, so the band grows like √h forever. To get that right for a differenced model you must expand the operator φ(B)(1−B)^d first, or your intervals will be far too narrow.
    
        
    function psiWeights(phi, theta, H) {
  const psi = new Array(H + 1).fill(0); psi[0] = 1;
  for (let j = 1; j <= H; j++) {
    let v = j <= theta.length ? theta[j-1] : 0;
    for (let i = 1; i <= Math.min(j, phi.length); i++) v += phi[i-1] * psi[j - i];
    psi[j] = v;
  }
  return psi;
}
// fold the differencing INTO the AR operator: phi(B) * (1-B)^d
function polyMulARd(phi, d) {
  let a = [1]; for (const f of phi) a.push(-f);          // a(B) = 1 - phi_1 B - ...
  for (let k = 0; k < d; k++) {                          // multiply by (1 - B)
    const out = new Array(a.length + 1).fill(0);
    for (let i = 0; i < a.length; i++) { out[i] += a[i]; out[i+1] -= a[i]; }
    a = out;
  }
  return a.slice(1).map(v => -v);
}

    AIC and BIC over a grid — and the two traps
    
        
    More parameters always fit the training data better, so residual variance cannot choose an order. AIC and BIC add a penalty: AIC = −2ℓ + 2k, BIC = −2ℓ + k·log n, with BIC punishing complexity harder as the sample grows and therefore picking sparser models. Two traps ruin this in practice and both are easy to hit. First, likelihoods are only comparable on the same data — so you may not compare AIC across different d, because differencing changes the dataset; pick d from a stationarity test, then grid p and q at that d. Second, a conditional fit burns startup observations, and a bigger model burns more, so unless you force every candidate onto a common effective sample you are comparing likelihoods computed from different numbers of points, and the grid will reward whichever model happened to get the easier subset.
    
        
    function infoCriteria(sse, n, k) {
  const s2 = sse / n;
  const ll = -0.5 * n * (Math.log(2 * Math.PI * s2) + 1);   // Gaussian, sigma2 profiled out
  return { s2, ll, aic: -2*ll + 2*k, bic: -2*ll + k*Math.log(n) };
}

function gridARIMA(y, d, pmax, qmax) {
  const z = differenceKeep(y, d).z;
  let burn = 0;                              // the SAME startup for every candidate
  for (let p = 0; p <= pmax; p++) for (let q = 0; q <= qmax; q++)
    burn = Math.max(burn, startupFor(z, p, q));
  const rows = [];
  for (let p = 0; p <= pmax; p++) for (let q = 0; q <= qmax; q++) {
    const m  = hannanRissanen(z, p, q);
    const cs = css(z, m, burn);              // conditional SSE over the COMMON window
    rows.push({ p, d, q, ...infoCriteria(cs.sse, cs.n, p + q + 2) });
  }
  return { rows, bestAIC: minBy(rows, r => r.aic), bestBIC: minBy(rows, r => r.bic) };
}

    Backtesting — beat the naive baseline or go home
    
        
    Never shuffle a time series into a random split; that trains on the future to predict the past and produces gorgeous, meaningless scores. The honest evaluation is rolling origin: fit on everything up to time t, forecast h ahead, score, advance t, refit, repeat. Then compare against baselines that cost nothing — naive (tomorrow equals today), drift (extend the straight line from first to last point), and seasonal naive (this month equals the same month last year). If your ARIMA cannot beat all three, it is not a model, it is decoration. On genuinely random-walk data the naive forecast is optimal and nothing can beat it, and knowing that is worth more than any grid search.
    
        
    function rollingOrigin(y, p, d, q, minTrain, h, season) {
  const em = [], en = [], es = [], ed = [];
  for (let t0 = minTrain; t0 + h <= y.length; t0++) {
    const train = y.slice(0, t0);                       // NOTHING after t0 is visible
    const fit = fitARIMA(train, p, d, q);               // REFIT from scratch every origin
    const fm = forecastARIMA(train, fit, h).mean;
    const fn = naiveForecast(train, h);                 // flat at the last value
    const fd = driftForecast(train, h);                 // straight line through the ends
    const fs = seasonalNaive(train, h, season);         // last season, repeated
    for (let i = 0; i < h; i++) {
      const a = y[t0 + i];
      em.push(a - fm[i]); en.push(a - fn[i]); ed.push(a - fd[i]); es.push(a - fs[i]);
    }
  }
  const R = e => Math.sqrt(e.reduce((s,v) => s + v*v, 0) / e.length);
  return { model: R(em), naive: R(en), drift: R(ed), snaive: R(es) };
}

🎉 Day 61 of MachineLearningFromZero done — the first model in this series where shuffling the rows destroys the data.
ARIMA is three letters and one precondition. Stationarity is the precondition — parameters learned from the past only transfer to the future if the mean, variance and autocorrelations do not depend on when you looked, and the ADF regression Δy_t = α + ρy_{t−1} + … is how you check rather than hope. I is the repair: ∇y_t = y_t − y_{t−1} applied d times kills trends and unit roots, and every forecast must be integrated back up by cumulative summation — an exactly invertible round trip and therefore the best test in the pipeline. ACF ρ_k = γ_k/γ_0 and the Durbin–Levinson PACF identify what is left against the ±1.96/√n band — AR(p) cuts the PACF at p, MA(q) cuts the ACF at q, ARMA cuts neither. AR is fitted two independent ways, a Toeplitz Yule–Walker solve and ordinary least squares on lagged rows, which agree in the limit and disagree in finite samples for a reason worth knowing. MA is harder because its regressors are unobserved shocks, so Hannan–Rissanen manufactures them from a long AR and then runs one more regression. Forecasting is a recursion fed its own output, which is why an AR(1) mean-reverts as φ^h and why the right forecast of a random walk is a flat line; the ψ-weights of φ(B)(1−B)^d give Var(e_h) = σ²Σψ_j² and a band that must widen. And nothing is real until a rolling-origin backtest beats naive, drift and seasonal-naive — AIC picks a shortlist, the backtest picks the model. 👉 Tomorrow — Day 62: continuing MachineLearningFromZero.
