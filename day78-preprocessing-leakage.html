🌱 Preprocessing leakage — fit the scaler after you split is the first rule anybody learns, and this measures what breaking it is actually worth. Nothing here is estimated: the estimator is a closed-form ridge regression solved by Gaussian elimination, and every score is the exact risk over a 4,000-row held-out population rather than a sample of one. 🔴 The rule everyone repeats guards the step that costs almost nothing. Fitting the scaler on all the data changes the true risk by at most 0.000168 across six seeds — under two ten-thousandths. Selecting features on all the data, which usually gets a footnote, is worth 0.6381 at minimum and 0.7814 at the seed shown here. 🔴 And what that buys is worse than a wrong number. On a population with no signal in it at all — 1,200 columns of pure noise, nothing to learn — the leaked pipeline reports 0.5370 where the honest one reports 1.0152. It looks like a model that halved the error. There is nothing there to halve. 🔴 The leaked model is also genuinely worse, 1.3184 against 1.2327, so you pay twice: a flattering number, and a model that earned less than the one you would have had.

The data is generated, and for this page that is a feature rather than an apology. Because the population is generated, the amount of real signal is known — and the headline setting has exactly none. Any performance a pipeline reports on it is, by construction, an artefact. That is not something a real dataset can give you.

Four pipelines, one population

“Reported” is the number the pipeline would put on a slide — its score on the validation split. “True risk” is the same fitted model scored on 4,000 rows it has never touched in any form. The gap between them is the whole subject.

1. The famous mistake is the cheap one

Fitting the scaler before the split leaks the mean and standard deviation of the held-out rows into the training set. It is the example in every tutorial. Six seeds, same population size:

The largest true-risk difference between the clean and scaler-leaked pipelines across all six seeds is 0.000168. Two means and two standard deviations, computed over a few extra rows, move the fitted model almost not at all. The rule is correct and worth keeping; the reason usually given for it — that it inflates your results — is not what the number says.

2. The mistake that pays is choosing what to look at

With 1,200 columns and 80 rows, some column correlates with the target by luck. Choosing the top fifteen using all the data means the validation rows helped choose the features they are about to score.

The optimism is never below 0.6381 on any seed. And this is on a population with zero signal columns — there is no relationship in the data to find, so every point of apparent performance is the selector reading its own homework.

3. You pay twice

It would be tidier if leakage only produced a flattering number for an equally good model. It does not.

The leaked model's true risk is worse than the clean model's. Selecting on the validation rows does not just misreport — it picks columns that fit noise those rows happened to carry, and that is a worse model to ship as well as a worse number to trust.

Where this generalises and where it does not

The ranking is what transfers: leakage through what you SELECT dominates leakage through what you SCALE, and it does so because selection has many degrees of freedom to exploit while standardisation has two numbers per column. The exact figures belong to this population. Turning the signal slider up does not shrink the gap, which is worth saying because it is what I expected. The coefficients grow with the signal count, so the target's variance grows and every mean-squared error grows with it. Worse for the tidy version of this page: above about four signal columns the comparison stops being stable at all — at signal 6 the clean pipeline is the more optimistic of the two, which is the opposite of the finding. With 80 rows and a 20-row validation split, the optimism of a single fit is a noisy quantity, and once the target carries real variance that noise swamps the leak. So the claim on this page is about the low-signal regime and is stated that way: p large, effect small, which is exactly where feature selection gets reached for. The ordering is checked at signal 0 and 2, and the reversal at 6 is checked too, so neither can quietly stop being true.

How it works

// clean
cols = topK(Xtrain, ytrain, k);   stats = stats(Xtrain[cols])

// the famous mistake            <- costs 0.000168
stats = stats(Xall[cols])

// the one that matters          <- costs 0.64 and up
cols  = topK(Xall, yall, k)

Ridge with a positive lambda, so the normal equations are always solvable and no comparison ever turns on a numerical accident. The population is 4,080 rows: 80 to work with, 4,000 held back and used only to compute the true risk. Draws come from a seeded Park-Miller generator, so every figure is reproducible.
