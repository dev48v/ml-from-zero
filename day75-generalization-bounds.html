📏 What does a generalization bound actually guarantee? — Day 73 asked what a clustering score is a number of and Day 74 asked which equalities can hold at once; both were about the number at the end of a run. This is about the number people put in front of one: the promise that a model which did well on the data it was fitted to will do well on data it has not seen. There is a whole theory of those promises, it is correct, and this page measures what it is worth. Nothing here is sampled: every labelling of every point set is enumerated, every sign pattern is summed over, and the learner’s true error is available in closed form, so “loose” is a factor and not an impression. Start with the capacity of a model class. Counted rather than bounded, the growth function matches its closed form on every size tried, for every class where one exists, and the VC dimension is read off the count — and the thing practitioners actually use instead, count the parameters, is right on 5 of 6 classes and then meets one with a single real parameter and infinite VC dimension, which realises all 4,096 labellings of 12 points from an explicit value each time. 🔴 The headline is the price. A class with three parameters trained on 2,000 examples carries a published guarantee of 24.07%; the same 100 examples held out instead certify 2.95% while knowing nothing whatever about the model, and matching that through the bound takes 196,214 training examples — 1,962× the labels for the same sentence. At a million parameters the bound is not loose, it is undefined until 18,428,117 examples and does not reach 10% until 3,994,311,196. And where the exact answer is computable the whole ladder can stand next to it: on the same learner, the same sample size and the same zero training error, the truth is 0.014771, an elementary union bound over the finite hypothesis set says 0.060034, the VC bound says 0.154085 — and the Rademacher bound built on the exact empirical Rademacher complexity, computed by dynamic programming over all 2m sign patterns rather than estimated, says 0.396076. 🔴 That last number is the belief this page was built around and it did not survive: replacing the complexity term by its exact value makes the bound worse than the one it was supposed to improve, at every sample size on the ladder, and the elementary count of hypotheses beats the VC bound at every one too. The bound is never violated — it is a theorem, and 7 worlds and 141 swept configurations confirm it — it is just that it cannot see the distribution, so it prints the identical 0.154085 in all seven while the exact guarantee runs from 0.011719 down to exactly zero in 4 of them. The control is not free either, and the number that finally makes both sides comparable is an identity rather than a comparison: certifying what 1,600 training labels bought takes 1,635 held-out ones, because the truth after m training examples and the certificate after n held-out ones are the same function — a label is worth the same on either side of the split, and only one side tells you the answer. 🔴 Then the control’s own failure, exact too: use those 100 held-out examples to pick the best of 20 models and a nominal 95% interval covers 61.88% of the time, and at 100 models 9.07% — but that collapse needs the models to be different, and at 50% shared errors it disappears completely, back to 100.00%. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

Every number below is either an exhaustive enumeration, a closed form evaluated deterministically, or an exact rational over BigInt. There is no dataset on this page and no generator: the growth functions count all 2m labellings, the Rademacher complexities sum over all 2m sign patterns, and the learner’s true error is the exact law of a maximum, computed as one integer over one common denominator. Where a closed form exists the enumeration is run anyway and the two are asserted equal, so the formulas here are checked rather than quoted. The problem is deliberately the friendliest one available: realizable, one dimension, and the hypothesis handed to the bound is the worst one consistent with the sample — exactly the quantity the realizable bound bounds.

What a guarantee guarantees

A model is fitted to data and scores well on it. The question everyone actually has is whether it will score well on the next batch, and there are exactly two ways to answer it. One is to reason about the model class before seeing anything — a generalization bound. The other is to keep some data back and look. This page puts both in the same units on the same problem and reads off the difference.

The theory is not wrong. Not once on this page, over seven worlds and a swept grid of configurations, does the true error exceed the bound. That is the point: it is a theorem, and theorems hold. The finding is about the size of the number it hands you.

1. Capacity, counted

The growth function Π(m) of a class is the largest number of distinct labellings it can produce on m points. It is usually quoted. Here it is counted: for each class and each size, all 2m labellings are generated and each is tested for realizability, and the count is compared with the closed form. Thresholds give m+1; one interval gives C(m,2)+m+1; a union of two intervals gives Σi≤4C(m,i); and half-planes give Cover’s function-counting number 2Σk≤2C(m−1,k). The enumeration agrees with every one of them at every size, as an equality of integers.

The half-plane row is worth pausing on, because the two routes have nothing in common. The points are placed on a parabola, so they are in general position, and a line meets a parabola at most twice — which means the positives are a contiguous run of indices or the complement of one, and the count is a small combinatorial argument about runs. Cover’s theorem gets the same integer from a completely different place: a recursion on the number of dichotomies when a point is added. They agree at every size.

The VC dimension is then not asserted, it is read off: it is the largest m at which the count reaches 2m. And the Sauer–Shelah lemma turns out to be exact rather than merely true for three of the classes — thresholds, intervals and unions of two intervals hit Σi≤dC(m,i) with equality at every size. Half-planes and boxes sit under it by factors the table prints.

2. The heuristic that scores five out of five

Nobody enumerates labellings before picking a model. What people use is: capacity is about the number of free parameters. On these classes it is not approximately right, it is exactly right — a threshold has one parameter and VC dimension 1, an interval two and 2, a union of two intervals four and 4, a half-plane in the plane three and 3, a box four and 4.

Then a sixth class. Let hθ(i) be the i-th binary digit of a single real number θ in [0,1). One parameter. Given any labelling of the points 0,1,…,m−1, write the labels down as the digits of θ and you have realised it. The page does exactly that: it builds the dyadic rational for each of the 2m labellings and reads the digits back one at a time, and every labelling is produced. One parameter, infinite VC dimension, and the heuristic that had just gone five for five is not merely wrong but unboundedly wrong.

This is not a curiosity about counting; it is why parameter counts stopped predicting generalization the moment models got large. The digit class is the clean version of the same fact.

3. Whose property is the growth function?

A smaller correction, and one this page had to make to its own framing. The growth function is a maximum over point sets, and for boxes that maximum is delicate: the same eleven points count 337 labellings when they sit in convex position and 67 on a diagonal. So “how many labellings can this model produce” is not a property of the model, it is a property of the model and of where the data happens to be — and the theory takes the worst case over the second half.

4. Rademacher, without the second bound

The modern route replaces the growth function with the empirical Rademacher complexity: flip a fair coin for every point, and ask how well the class can correlate with pure noise. It is a genuinely better object — it depends on the actual sample rather than on the worst one — and it is almost always then bounded again, by Massart’s lemma, back in terms of the growth function.

Here it is computed exactly, so the second bound is not needed. For the threshold class the supremum over hypotheses of Σσih(xi) is the largest suffix sum of the sign vector, which is the running maximum of a plus/minus-one walk, and the reflection principle gives P(Mm≥a) = P(Sm≥a) + P(Sm≥a+1) — an exact rational with denominator 2m. A dynamic program over the sign patterns reproduces it; at small sizes so does the definition, summed over every pattern. For one interval the supremum is the largest contiguous sum, which is Kadane’s quantity, and its exact law comes from a dynamic program over (best ending here, best so far).

🔴 And this is where the page lost the claim it was built around. Feeding the exact complexity into the Rademacher bound was supposed to show what the theory can do when it stops throwing accuracy away. It produces the loosest guarantee on the ladder, at every sample size — worse than the VC bound it was meant to improve on. The tightening lives in the complexity term, and the complexity term is not where the loss is. The constants and the √(1/m) deviation term are.

The digit class settles the same point differently: since it contains every labelling, its supremum is the sum of the positive signs, so its empirical Rademacher complexity is exactly 1/2 at every sample size — the largest value the quantity can take — and its Rademacher bound is above 1 forever. That is correct behaviour. A class that can fit any labelling of your data has nothing to promise you.

5. The exact answer, so “loose” is a number

Take the friendliest learning problem that exists. The domain is a finite grid, the target labels everything negative, and the learner returns a threshold consistent with the sample. Every consistent hypothesis predicts positive above some cut greater than the largest point seen, so the worst consistent hypothesis is the one that cuts immediately above it, and its true error is exactly the mass sitting above the largest point the sample happened to contain.

That makes the entire distribution of the true error a closed form in the CDF, with no sample anywhere:

P(error > 1 - F(j))  =  F(j-1)^m
E[error]  =  sum_j (1 - F(j)) * (F(j)^m - F(j-1)^m)      // over the values it takes
          =  sum_x p(x) * F(x-1)^m                        // over the points, swapping the sums

Two different sums over two different index sets. Written over the common denominator Dm+1 they are two integers, and the page asserts they are the same integer — not the same to within a tolerance, the same. The (1−δ) quantile is then a monotone integer comparison, so the exact guarantee is available by binary search.

Now the comparison the page exists for. On the same learner, the same sample and the same zero training error, four guarantees and one truth. The truth is smallest, then an elementary union bound over the finite hypothesis set, then the VC bound, then the Rademacher bound, then the agnostic VC bound. The elementary bound — take the number of hypotheses, divide the failure probability among them, done in one line — beats the VC bound at every sample size on the ladder. Everything more sophisticated than counting is buying generality, not tightness.

6. What the bound is blind to

The bound is a function of the VC dimension, the sample size, the confidence and the training error. It contains no term for the data. Seven worlds that differ only in how the mass is spread across the domain therefore receive the identical number, while the exact 95% guarantee runs from a percent or so down to exactly zero in the concentrated ones — zero, because with high probability the top atom is drawn and there is then nothing left to be wrong about.

It would be easy to stop there and say the bound is loose because it cannot see the distribution. That is the second claim this page had to correct. For an atomless law the expected error of the worst consistent hypothesis is exactly 1/(m+1) — whatever the shape of the distribution, because F(X) is uniform and the whole problem is invariant. The page refines the grid four times and watches the exact value climb towards 1/(m+1) from below, always inside one atom of it. So the spread across those seven worlds is entirely a discreteness effect. The bound’s blindness to the distribution is real, and it is not the reason it is loose.

7. A label is worth the same on either side of the split

The control for a bound is not another bound, it is a held-out set. With n held-out examples and zero mistakes on them, the exact Clopper–Pearson upper limit solves (1−U)n = δ, so U = 1 − δ1/n. It knows nothing about the model, the class, the algorithm or the number of parameters. It is the same number for a threshold and for a transformer.

Set that beside the exact truth for this problem, which is 1 − δ1/m up to the grid resolution. The two are the same function. Certifying what m training labels bought takes about m held-out ones — the page computes the number and it lands one or two above m every time. So held-out data is not free, and anyone who tells you a test set is cheap has not priced it. But the exchange rate is one to one, and only one of the two sides can be read.

What is not one to one is the bound. To reach what 100 held-out examples certify, the agnostic VC bound at three parameters needs 196,214 training examples. At a thousand parameters it needs 58,179,197. At a million, the bound is not defined at all until 18,428,117 examples, because a formula containing ln(2m/d) has nothing to say while m < d — which is the regime every deployed model of the last decade has lived in.

8. Then the control breaks, in the way everyone breaks it

A held-out interval is a promise about one number measured once. The moment k models are scored on the same held-out set and the best is reported, the promise is about the minimum of k draws, and the exact binomial law says what is left of it. At twenty models a nominal 95% interval covers about three fifths of the time; at a hundred, under one tenth. The reported error rate is biased low by a computable amount, which is the winner’s curse arriving in the one place on this page that was supposed to be safe.

🔴 And that collapse has a condition, which is the third correction. It needs the models to be different. Hold the number at twenty and make them fail on the same items instead — a shared hard core plus an independent remainder, with the total error rate held fixed — and the coverage climbs back, reaching the nominal level again well before the errors are fully shared. Selection can only exploit variation that exists. A leaderboard of near-identical checkpoints is much safer than a leaderboard of genuinely different ideas, which is not a comfortable thing for a leaderboard to be.

9. What to take away

Not that the theory is useless. It tells you which quantities matter, it is what makes “capacity” a definable thing at all, and the shape of the bounds — the √(d/m), the ln(1/δ) — is how anyone knows what to expect when a knob is turned. What it is not is a number to put in a report.

The operational version is short. A guarantee computed from the model class is a statement about every dataset you might have had; a guarantee computed from held-out data is a statement about the one you did have. The second is smaller, it costs about as many labels as the first pretends to need none of, and it is destroyed by exactly one thing — looking twice.

10. Verification

The block between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END is DOM-free, clock-free and free of any random source; it is extracted by regex and run under Node against assertions written independently of it. The oracles share no code with the page: growth functions recounted by a different realizability test (convex-hull separation with integer orientation predicates, instead of the run-counting shortcut); Cover’s formula rebuilt from its recursion rather than its closed form; Sauer–Shelah recomputed from BigInt factorials; the Rademacher complexities re-derived by exhaustive enumeration over every sign pattern and by a forward simulator with a different generator reported as a confidence interval around the exact value; the exact error law rebuilt by dynamic programming over the sample maximum and, at small sizes, by enumerating every one of the Nm samples; the Clopper–Pearson limit re-solved from an exact BigInt rational binomial tail rather than from logarithms; the reuse coverage recomputed by exact convolution; and every bound re-evaluated from its published algebraic form. On top of that a mutation harness corrupts the engine in targeted ways and requires the verifier to notice every one.

🔨 How it is built

1. No sampling, anywhere

The rule for this run is that ground truth has to be a closed form or an exact computation. That is unusually easy to honour here, because everything in learning theory that this page touches is either a count or an order statistic. Growth functions are counts. Rademacher complexities are averages over 2m equally likely sign vectors, so they are counts divided by 2m. The learner’s true error is a function of the sample maximum, and the law of a maximum is Fm. None of those needs a draw.

Rationals are {n, d} over BigInt, reduced by Euclid, with equality as a.n*b.d === b.n*a.d. Where the numbers get large the reduction is skipped deliberately: the exact-error law is computed as integers over one shared denominator Dm+1, because a greatest common divisor of two twenty-thousand-digit integers costs more than the rest of the page put together, and comparing two fractions with the same denominator does not need one.

2. Realizability tests that are arguments

Each class is a predicate on a bit mask. The predicates are short because the geometry has been done first, and each one is a theorem rather than a heuristic:

threshold   : the positives are a SUFFIX of the sorted points
interval    : the positives form at most ONE run
2 intervals : at most TWO runs
half-plane  : one run, OR the complement is one run
              (points on a parabola; a line meets it at most twice)
box         : every point inside the bounding box of the positives is positive
digits      : always - build theta from the labels and read it back

The parabola is the trick that makes half-planes exactly enumerable without a linear program. Because y = x² is convex and a line crosses it at most twice, the set of points below any line is an interval of indices and the set above is the complement of one. The verifier does not take that on trust: it re-tests separability with integer convex-hull predicates and gets the same masks.

3. The reflection principle instead of a bigger loop

Summing over all 2m sign patterns is fine at m = 12 and hopeless at m = 400. Two escapes are used, and they are checked against each other and against the definition. The dynamic program carries the pair (running statistic, best so far) as a state and propagates exact BigInt counts, which is O(m3) and covers the middle range. The closed form covers everything:

P(M_m >= a) = P(S_m >= a) + P(S_m >= a+1)          a >= 1
E[M_m]      = sum_{a=1..m} P(M_m >= a)
            = (1/2^m) * sum_a ( T[ceil((m+a)/2)] + T[ceil((m+a+1)/2)] )

where T is the tail sum of binomial coefficients, precomputed once. That is O(m) BigInt additions for an expectation over 2400 patterns.

4. Bounds evaluated, never clipped

Each bound is written down in its published algebraic form and evaluated. A value above 1 is printed above 1, because that is the finding rather than an error; a formula whose logarithm has the wrong sign when m < d returns undefined instead of a number, and the table says so. “The smallest sample size at which this bound first drops below x” is answered by bisection on a monotone function, so those five- and ten-digit numbers are derived rather than typed.

5. Clopper–Pearson as an equation, not a table

The exact upper limit is the U solving P(Bin(n,U) ≤ k) = δ. The left side is monotone decreasing in U, so a hundred bisection steps solve it to machine precision, and at k = 0 the equation collapses to (1−U)n = δ, which is a closed form. The page asserts the two agree, which is a test of the solver rather than a claim about statistics.

The reuse section then needs the smallest error count whose interval still covers the truth. That is monotone in the count, so it is another bisection — eight interval solves instead of n of them — and the coverage is P(min of k counts ≥ that), which is a tail probability raised to the k.

6. What is asserted

Bounds and identities, never constants. “The enumerated growth function equals the closed form” is an integer identity; “the growth function is under the Sauer–Shelah ceiling” is an integer inequality; “the two routes to the mean error agree” is an equality of two integers of several thousand digits; “the exact guarantee never exceeds the bound” is the theorem, asserted in every world and every swept configuration. Nothing on the page asserts that a particular float equals a particular decimal, because two summation orders differ at 1e-16 and an assertion that cannot survive that is a trap rather than a test — Day 68 lost an afternoon to exactly that.

7. Cost

A full render enumerates a few tens of thousands of labellings, runs three dynamic programs, raises a few thousand BigInts to a power and solves a few dozen bisections. It takes a couple of seconds, so slider drags are debounced rather than raced.
