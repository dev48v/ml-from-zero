🔒 What does ε actually buy? — Day 73 asked what a clustering score is a number of, Day 74 which fairness equalities can hold at once, and Day 75 what the guarantee printed in front of a training run is worth. All three were promises about a model. This one is the promise attached to the data: the claim that a release is safe to publish. Nothing here is sampled either — the mechanisms live on databases small enough to enumerate completely, so “this release is ε-differentially private” is checked against its own definition over all 2,048 neighbouring pairs and every output, as an exact rational over BigInt, and the attack on it is a finite sum over all 256 databases rather than a simulation. Start with the definition. The closed form ln(1/α) is confirmed to the last integer for the bounded geometric mechanism — and the version almost everyone writes first, geometric noise truncated to the valid range and renormalised, is not ε-differentially private at the ε it advertises: it comes back at 1.32× the claim, the excess has a closed form of its own, and it does not go away as the database grows. 🔴 Then the price list. The privacy loss of the correct mechanism turns out to be a two-point law, exactly +ε or −ε and nothing in between, so the average leak is exactly (1−α)/(1+α) of the guarantee — 0.2310 nats against a promise of 0.6931 — and anyone reporting the average is quoting a number the definition never made. Publishing only totals buys nothing whatsoever: releasing every noisy bit and releasing only their sum have the same worst-case ratio, the same integer. 🔴 Then the attack, and the control it is measured against. Publish 10 exact subgroup counts over 12 people — no names, no identifiers, nothing but totals — and 100.0% of the rows are pinned down uniquely; round every count to the nearest 3 and the attacker still recovers 80.70% of the private bits; suppress every cell below 3, which is the rule actually written into disclosure policies, and it recovers 97.36%. 🔴 And the headline is the one this page was not built to find. On the same file, at the same 5 queries, rounding to the nearest 7 has a lower mean squared error than the ε-differentially private mechanism at 3.47 — 1.0000 against 1.1769 — and lets the attacker recover fewer bits, 61.91% against 65.43%. The release with the infinite ε beats the private one on both measured axes, which is not what I expected to be writing. The column where it never wins is the one the definition is actually about: rounding pins 13.82% of individual bits with certainty — posterior exactly one — and every ε-private mechanism on the page pins exactly zero, with the largest posterior it ever reaches sitting under eε/(1+eε) in every row and hitting it with equality at the mechanism that releases nothing. An attack is one adversary. The guarantee is all of them. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

There is no dataset and no generator on this page. A world is the set of all 2n databases at once, every probability is an exact rational over BigInt, and every headline is either an exhaustive enumeration or a closed form — where a closed form exists the enumeration is run anyway and the two are asserted equal as integers. The attacker is not a heuristic: given a release it holds the exact Bayes posterior over all 2n databases under a uniform prior and guesses each bit by its posterior majority, so “how much did this leak” is a finite sum and not a simulation. Chance is exactly 50%.

2 · the truncation everybody writes first is not private at its own ε

Discrete noise has to land somewhere legal. The obvious repair is to keep only the outputs inside 0..n and renormalise, which is a probability distribution and looks harmless. It is not: the normalising constant depends on the input, and a count in the middle of the range has more room for noise than a count at the end. The residue has a closed form, Z1/Z0, and it survives to infinity.

Randomised response flips every person’s bit and publishes the result. Publishing the noisy bits feels reckless and publishing only their noisy total feels responsible. Both worst-case ratios are enumerated, and they are the same rational number, not two numbers that happen to round alike.

What a privacy guarantee guarantees

Somebody wants to publish a number computed from data about people. The number is a count, an average, a model, a dashboard. The question is whether publishing it hurts anybody in the file, and there are two ways to answer it. One is to attack your own release and see what comes back. The other is to prove a bound on what any attack could come back with. This page runs both on the same tiny world and reads off the difference, and the difference is not the one I expected.

1. The definition is a maximum

A randomised release M is ε-differentially private if for every pair of databases D, D′ that differ in one person, and every possible output o,

P(M(D) = o)  <=  e^eps * P(M(D') = o)

Three quantifiers, no distribution over databases, no assumption about what the attacker knows. That is the whole reason the definition is worth anything: it does not depend on the attacker being weak, or on the data being typical, or on the auxiliary information the attacker might already hold. It is also why it is expensive, and why every shortcut people take instead turns out to be a different statement.

The mechanics of small worlds make it checkable. With n people carrying one private bit each there are 2n databases and n·2n neighbouring ordered pairs, and if the output space is finite too then the definition is a finite maximum over a finite table of exact rationals. So on this page “this mechanism is ε-DP” is never quoted. It is enumerated.

2. The first implementation is wrong

Noise on a count has to be discrete, and it has to land in a legal range. The two-sided geometric distribution — the discrete Laplace — puts mass ∝ α|k−y| on output y, and unbounded, it is exactly ln(1/α)-private. Truncate it to 0..n and renormalise, and the distribution is still perfectly well defined, still centred on the truth, still has the same shape. It is no longer ln(1/α)-private, because the normalising constant Zk = Σyα|y−k| is larger for a k in the middle of the range than for a k at the end. The ratio picks up an extra factor Zk+1/Zk, worst at the boundary, and the page evaluates it in closed form and confirms it against the enumeration.

The damage does not shrink with n. It converges upward to ln(1+α−α2), an extra 0.22 nats at α = 1/2, so a mechanism sold as ε = 0.69 is actually 0.92. The repair is not more noise. It is where the truncated mass goes: pile it on the two endpoints instead of redistributing it proportionally, which is the bounded geometric mechanism, and the enumeration returns ln(1/α) as an exact rational identity at every α and every n tried.

3. Aggregating is not a privacy measure

“We never publish row-level data, only totals.” Randomised response makes that testable, because the noisy bits and the noisy total are the same mechanism with a post-processing step between them. Post-processing cannot increase the loss — that is a one-line theorem — but here it does not decrease it either. Both enumerations return the identical rational (1−p)/p. The worst case is achieved at the extreme total, where the sum is as informative about the one changed bit as the whole vector was.

That does not mean aggregation is useless. It means aggregation is a utility decision, not a privacy one, and putting it in the privacy column is how a release that leaks everything gets described as safe.

4. The average leak is the wrong summary

For the bounded geometric mechanism, the privacy loss random variable ln(P(o|D)/P(o|D′)) takes exactly two values, +ε and −ε. There is no middle. The mean of that variable is the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the two output laws, and it works out to ε·(1−α)/(1+α) exactly — a third of the guarantee at α = 1/2, and a nineteenth of it at α = 9/10.

So a report that measures “the average information leaked per query” and finds 0.0055 nats is not measuring a weaker version of ε. It is measuring a different object, one that stays small precisely as the tail gets more dangerous, and the gap between them widens as the mechanism gets more private. Interestingly the same expression, (1−α)/(1+α), is the total variation distance between the two output laws, so the “how often can you even tell” number and the “how much do you learn on average” number coincide here.

5. The attack: aggregates are a linear system

Nothing in the reconstruction uses identifiers, because there are none. The attacker is given only the sizes of subgroups and the published totals within them. Each published count is one equation in the private bits; enough equations, and the file is the unique solution. That is Dinur and Nissim’s observation, and on a small world it needs no theorem, because the solution set can be enumerated: bucket all 2n databases by what they would have published, and the attacker’s posterior is uniform on the bucket it lands in.

With exact counts and about as many published subgroups as people, every bucket is a singleton and every row is identified. Rounding does not stop it and neither does small-cell suppression, because “this cell was suppressed” is itself an inequality constraint, not a redaction — and it is a constraint on the small cells, which are the ones that pin individuals down. Suppressing below three, on the default settings here, still gives the attacker over 97% of the private bits.

6. Then the finding this page was not built for

🔴 The obvious next table is the frontier: privacy against utility, on one file, with the same attacker. It does not say what I assumed it would. Round every published count to the nearest seven — which on a subgroup of four people is barely a release at all — and it costs a mean squared error of 1.0000 and hands the attacker 61.91% of the bits. The ε-private mechanism at a total budget of 3.47 costs more error, 1.1769, and hands the attacker more bits, 65.43%. On both measured axes, the release with an infinite ε wins.

That is not a bug in either mechanism and it is not an argument against differential privacy. It is a fact about what a reconstruction score measures. Average bit recovery under a uniform prior is one adversary with one prior against all targets at once; it is an average of averages, and a coarse deterministic release can score well on it while being catastrophic for the particular people it happens to expose. The column that shows this is the one the definition is actually about: how many individual bits are pinned down with certainty. Rounding to the nearest seven pins 13.8% of them — posterior exactly one, no doubt at all, for one person in seven. Every ε-private row on the page pins exactly zero, and that zero is structural rather than lucky: a full-support mechanism can never produce a degenerate posterior.

So the operational reading is uncomfortable in both directions. You cannot certify a release by attacking it, because a release can survive your attack and still be publishing individual people with certainty. And you cannot dismiss ε because a particular attack scores badly against it, because the attack is not what ε is a bound on.

7. What ε is a bound on

Under a prior that makes the rows independent, an ε-private release moves the posterior odds on any individual bit by a factor of at most eε. With a uniform prior that caps the posterior at eε/(1+eε), and the page checks that cap against the largest posterior the full enumeration finds. It holds in every row, it is reasonably tight, and at the mechanism that publishes a constant it holds with exact equality at one half, which is the sanity anchor: the definition and the attack agree exactly where there is nothing to disagree about.

Read the bound backwards and it prices the budget honestly. At a total ε of 3.47 the cap is 0.97 — the release is allowed to take an attacker from a coin flip to 97% confidence about a specific person. That is what a budget of 3.47 buys, and the reason large budgets are not obviously fine is not that the theorem gets weaker; it is that the theorem was never promising much at that number.

8. Composition, and the control that wins

Run k private releases with fresh randomness and the budgets add. Not approximately: the page enumerates the joint output space for small k and the worst-case ratio is the single-query ratio raised to the k-th power, as an exact integer identity. So a dashboard of a hundred panels, each individually reassuring at ε = 0.1, is one release at ε = 10, which permits a likelihood ratio of 22,026 on any individual.

The standard escape is advanced composition, which grows like √k instead of k at the cost of a δ. It is a real theorem and the page evaluates it, and the honest result is that for the per-query budgets people actually use it does not help. At ε = ln 2 per query it never beats plain addition at any k up to a million. At ε = 0.1 it takes until the thirty-fifth query. The control — add them up — is the right answer for most of the range, and it is also the pessimistic one, which is an unusual combination.

9. And δ

The relaxed definition allows the multiplicative bound to fail on an event of probability δ. There is a mechanism that satisfies it and does nothing else: with probability δ, publish the whole database. The page confirms by enumeration that its exact shortfall at every multiplicative bound, including e0 = 1, is exactly δ — so it is (0, δ)-private, the best ε there is, while publishing everything about everybody δ of the time. At δ = 10−6 over a million people that is one expected total exposure per release, which is the arithmetic behind the usual advice that δ must be much smaller than one over the number of records.

10. Verification

The block between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END is DOM-free, clock-free and free of any random source; it is extracted by regex and run under Node against assertions written independently of it. The oracles share no code with the page: an independent rational library with lazy reduction, so agreement is an integer cross-multiplication and never a tolerance; every mechanism’s law rebuilt from its defining recurrence rather than its formula; the worst-case ratio re-derived by a forward Monte-Carlo simulator with its own generator, reported as a bound in standard errors around the exact value; the attacker’s posterior recomputed by exhaustive enumeration over every output vector in a different loop order, and independently by a forward simulator that draws databases and outputs and counts hits; the composition identity proved by expanding the product space; the reconstruction cross-checked by exact integer linear algebra over the published equations; the binomial weights rebuilt from BigInt factorials; and a mutation harness that corrupts the engine in targeted ways and requires the verifier to notice every one.

🔨 How it is built

1. The world is every database at once

The rule for this run is that ground truth is a closed form or an exact computation, never an estimate. Differential privacy makes that easy in a way most topics do not, because the definition is already a finite maximum: with n people holding one bit each there are 2n databases, n·2n neighbouring ordered pairs, and a finite output space, so ε is the largest entry in a table you can build.

The attacker is exact for the same reason. Under a uniform prior the posterior over databases given an output is proportional to the likelihood, so the Bayes-optimal guess for person i’s bit is whichever value carries more posterior mass, and the expected accuracy is

acc = (1/(n * 2^n)) * sum_over_outputs sum_over_databases
        P(o|D) * [ majority_i(o) == D_i ]

which is a finite sum with no draws in it. Ties are scored at one half, which is what an attacker with no tie-break rule actually gets.

2. Two attackers, and they have to agree

A deterministic release admits a much cheaper attack: bucket the databases by what they would publish, and the posterior is uniform on the bucket. That is O(2nm) instead of O(|O|m2n), which is what makes the fourteen-person ladder affordable. Both attackers are implemented and the deterministic rows are run through both, with an assertion that they return the identical rational. A bug in the expensive one that the cheap one does not share would have to survive that.

3. Integer weights over one denominator

The expensive attack multiplies m probabilities per database per output vector. Doing that in reduced rationals would spend all its time in gcd. Instead every mechanism’s law is converted once into integer weights over a single common denominator shared by every input, so a likelihood is a product of BigInts and the posterior comparison is an integer comparison. The common denominator has to be common across inputs, not per input — the first version of this used a per-input denominator and the posterior silently reweighted databases by their count, which is the kind of bug that produces a plausible-looking table where noise beats exact answers.

4. Mechanisms as distributions, not samplers

Every mechanism is a function from an input count to a list of {output, probability}, with the probability an exact rational. Deterministic releases are the special case with one output at probability one, which means rounding, suppression and “publish nothing” go through the same ε-enumerator as the noisy ones rather than being declared infinite. That is load-bearing: it is what turns up that publishing a constant has ε exactly zero, and it is why the frontier table has an anchor row where the bound is met with equality.

bounded geometric, alpha = a/b, range 0..h

  P(0 | k) = alpha^k       / (1+alpha)
  P(h | k) = alpha^(h-k)   / (1+alpha)
  P(y | k) = alpha^|y-k| * (1-alpha)/(1+alpha)     0 < y < h

naive truncation, same shape, renormalised per input

  P(y | k) = alpha^|y-k| / Z_k        Z_k = sum_y alpha^|y-k|
  worst ratio = (1/alpha) * Z_1/Z_0   <-- the extra factor, in closed form

5. Query sets without a random source

The published subgroups have to look arbitrary without being random, because the engine has no Math.random and no clock. Each subgroup is chosen by a fixed integer mixer over (seed, query index, person index): hash every person, sort, take the first h. Duplicate subgroups are rejected by bumping a salt. The first version drew each membership bit from a single XOR of three multiples, and it collapsed — twenty “different” subgroups were two distinct sets repeated, the reconstruction accuracy was flat in the number of queries, and the flatness is what gave it away. A hash that has to look random needs an avalanche step, not just a multiply.

6. What is asserted

Bounds and identities, never constants. “The enumerated worst-case ratio equals 1/α” is an equality of two BigInt cross-products; “ε composes by exactly k” is a rational raised to a power; “no posterior exceeds eε/(1+eε)” is an inequality checked in every row of every sweep; “a full-support mechanism pins nothing” is an exact zero. Nothing asserts that a particular float equals a particular decimal, because two summation orders differ at 1e-16 and an assertion that cannot survive that is a trap and not a test — Day 68 lost an afternoon to exactly that, and this page has one place where the same thing nearly happened: the naive truncation’s closed form is compared to the enumeration as rationals, not as logarithms, because the logarithms agree only to about 1e-15.

7. Cost

A full render enumerates a few hundred thousand neighbouring pairs, buckets sixteen thousand databases against two dozen releases, and runs the exact attack over roughly three thousand output vectors for each of a dozen mechanisms. It takes a second or two, so slider drags are debounced rather than raced, and the exactly enumerated head-to-head is deliberately held at eight people: |O|m·2n is the wall, and it is a steep one.
