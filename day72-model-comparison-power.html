📏 Is the test set big enough? — days 66 to 71 were an identification arc: given this file, what can be known at all. This one steps out, because six is enough and the question underneath was starting to repeat. Here the data does not exist yet and the only question is how much of it to collect. Two models, the same n test items, a 2×2 table of who got what right — and accA − accB = p₁₀ − p₀₁, so the two cells where both models agree cancel and carry no information about the difference whatsoever. Condition on the d rows where they disagreed and the null is Binomial(d, ½) exactly, which makes power a finite sum rather than a simulation: at n = 600 it is 0.830230251229, computed by two different routes that agree to 3.0e-14 — and worth having exactly, because 2,000 replications read 0.8415, which is 1.3σ away and would have shipped. That number does not depend on how good the models are. Five worlds whose accuracies run from 97.6% down to 30.8% share one power to the last digit, and the sampler cannot tell them apart: 60,000 row comparisons, 0 differences, while accuracy spans 0.7117. The unpaired design — score A on one test set and B on another — has no such invariance: same gap, same α, and it needs from 2.3× to 15.3× the items, with the multiplier set by an identity the page asserts to 1.1e-16, Varunpaired − Varpaired = 2(p₁₁p₀₀ − p₁₀p₀₁) — twice the determinant of that 2×2 table, which is zero at exactly two accuracies and nowhere else. You ask for α = 0.05 and the machine spends 0.0331; you ask for 80% power, the textbook formula hands you n = 521, and 521 rows deliver 76.43% — the exact answer is 562. The conditional level ladder dips 215 times over d = 1 to 400 and the exact power dips 0 times over 2,999 consecutive sample sizes, which is not what I expected to find. Then the other way people compare models: k folds share a training set, so the per-fold differences are correlated, and the naive paired t-test is exactly √c times a real t variate with c = (1+(k−1)ρ)/(1−ρ) — a nominal 5% test that rejects a true null 51.24% of the time at k = 10, ρ = 0.5, rising to 65.30% at k = 20, because more folds is worse. And a default of ρ = 0 would have made the correction look like a no-op to 0e+0. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

How much test data buys an answer?

0. Breaking the arc on purpose

Days 66 to 71 were one question asked six ways. Cross-validation leakage, distribution shift, missing data, measurement error, label noise, selection on the outcome — all of them ask what can this file identify, and all of them end in a bound. Day 71 already strained it: its bound could not come from the data at all and had to come from the design. That is the crack this page walks through. Here nothing is unidentified. The estimand is trivial, the estimator is a difference of two counts, and the only question is whether the experiment is big enough — which is not an identification question, it is a design question, and it has an exact answer rather than an interval. Six identification days in a row is a lot, and the honest reason to stop is that the seventh would have been the same shape.

1. Two cells do all the work

Score two models on the same n items and every item lands in one of four cells: both right, only A right, only B right, both wrong. Then

acc_A - acc_B = (p11 + p10) - (p11 + p01) = p10 - p01

and p₁₁ has cancelled. So has p₀₀, which never appeared. Whatever the two models have in common — and for two decent models that is nearly everything — is irrelevant to the difference between them. A test set of 600 items on which the models disagree 36 times is a test of 36 items wearing a suit; the other 564 rows are already spent before the comparison starts. This is not an approximation or a large-sample argument. It is arithmetic on the definition of accuracy.

2. Conditioning makes the null exact

Under the null the two models are equally accurate, which says p₁₀ = p₀₁ and nothing about the other two cells. Condition on the number of disagreements d = n₁₀ + n₀₁ and each disagreement is a fair coin: n₁₀ | d ~ Binomial(d, ½), exactly, with no nuisance parameter left in the problem. That is McNemar's exact test. The chi-square version everyone quotes, (n₁₀−n₀₁)²/d, is a normal approximation to this and inherits none of its exactness.

Because the null is exact, the alternative is exact too. Under (p₁₀, p₀₁) the number of disagreements is Binomial(n, δ) and the split within them is Binomial(d, θ) with θ = p₁₀/δ, so power is a double sum with no simulation anywhere in it. The MEASURE tab computes it twice — once by conditioning and once as a flat sum of trinomial probabilities over the rejection region — and the two agree to about 3e-14, which is summation order and nothing else.

3. Power does not know how good the models are

Power is a function of (n, p₁₀, p₀₁, α). There is no p₁₁ in it. Two models at 97.6% and 94.6% are exactly as separable as two at 30.8% and 27.8%, provided they disagree the same way, and the page demonstrates it by construction rather than by a plot: the sampler takes the two discordant probabilities and nothing else, the concordant rows are painted afterwards by a different function from a different stream, and across five worlds spanning 0.71 in accuracy the test's input is the same integers on 60,000 row comparisons.

This is a genuinely useful fact and also a trap. It means a benchmark result of the form “A beats B by 3 points, p < 0.05, n = 600” carries no information about whether the models are any good, and a reader who assumes the significance was easier to reach because the accuracies were high has it backwards — it was neither easier nor harder.

4. The level is a ladder, not a number

At d disagreements the achievable two-sided levels are 2P(X ≤ c) for integer c: at d = 8 the choices are 0.0078 and 0.0703, so a 5% test spends 0.78%; at d = 17 there is a rung at 0.0490 and it spends almost all of it. Below d = 6 there is no rung at all and the test cannot reject at 5% no matter how lopsided the disagreements are — five out of five is p = 0.0625. Averaging that ladder over the random d gives the size actually spent, and it is strictly below α at every sample size: at the default, 0.0331 against a nominal 0.05. The test is not calibrated at 5%; it is conservative, and the missing third of the level is missing power that no amount of extra data recovers, because the discreteness lives in d.

5. The sawtooth that is not there

Given that ladder, I expected the exact power to be visibly non-monotone in n — that the classic discreteness sawtooth would be right there in the sample-size curve, and that “the smallest n reaching 80% power is not the smallest n above it” would be one of the findings. It is not. Over 2,999 consecutive sample sizes the exact power dips zero times and the exact size dips zero times, while the ladder they are built from dips 215 times over d = 1 to 400. The reason is that d is itself random: at n = 600 the number of disagreements has a standard deviation of about six, so every sample size averages the ladder over a dozen rungs and the teeth cancel. The sawtooth is real, it is just not observable in n. That is a negative result and it is on the page because it is the opposite of what I set out to show.

6. The textbook n is too small

The standard formula is a normal approximation to the same conditional argument:

n = [ z(1-a/2) sqrt(delta) + z(power) sqrt(delta - Delta^2) ]^2 / Delta^2

At the default it returns 521. Run the exact power at 521 and it is 0.7643, not 0.80; the exact requirement is 562, about 8% more. The direction is always the same — the formula ignores the conservatism of section 4 and therefore always under-books — and the size of the miss is worst in the middle, when the expected number of disagreements is a few dozen. It is small at very low disagreement rates because there the power is dominated by simply having enough discordant rows to reach the first rung of the ladder at all, and it shrinks again at high ones because the ladder gets dense. Nobody is going to be ruined by 8%, but it is a systematic 8% in the direction of an under-powered study, and the exact number costs a few milliseconds to compute.

7. More disagreement costs more data

This one I had backwards for an hour. Hold the accuracy gap at 3 points and vary how often the two models disagree at all. Intuition says two models that agree on almost everything are hard to tell apart. The arithmetic says the opposite: at δ = 0.03 — the minimum, where every single disagreement is one A wins — the exact requirement is 263 items, and at δ = 0.24 it is over two thousand. The gap is the signal and the disagreement rate is the noise: a disagreement A wins is evidence, a disagreement B wins is counter-evidence, and what the test measures is the imbalance between the two. Two models that differ only on the rows where A is right are the easiest possible comparison, not the hardest.

The practical reading is uncomfortable for benchmark practice: an ensemble and its base model, or a fine-tune and its checkpoint, are usually in exactly this happy regime and need surprisingly little test data. Two independently trained architectures with the same headline accuracy disagree constantly, and separating them at 3 points takes thousands of items.

8. What pairing is worth, exactly

The alternative design is to score A on one test set and B on another. Its variance is accA(1−accA) + accB(1−accB). The paired variance is δ − Δ². Subtract:

Var_unpaired - Var_paired = 2 (p11 p00 - p10 p01)

which is twice the determinant of the 2×2 table — that is, twice the covariance between the two models' correctness. The page asserts it to 1.1e-16 in every world and over a 201-point sweep. Two consequences. First, the saving is large: at the default world the unpaired variance is 0.2168 and the paired one is 0.0591, a factor of 3.7, and since the unpaired design needs two arms the item count runs 6.7×. Second, and this is the part the invariance in section 3 sets up, the unpaired requirement depends on the accuracies while the paired one does not: 639 items per arm when the models are at 97.6%, 4,314 when they are at 56.2%, because p(1−p) peaks at a half. So the answer to “how big a test set do I need” is a single number under pairing and a whole function under independence.

9. Two knife edges

The determinant is zero when the two models' errors are independent, and then paired and unpaired coincide exactly. Solving p₁₁p₀₀ = p₁₀p₀₁ for the concordant split gives two roots and the page prints both: at the default they sit at accuracies of about 4.6% and 98.4%, and at those two points the variance ratio is 1.0000 to twelve decimals. Everywhere between them the determinant is positive and pairing wins. It is worth knowing that the knife edge exists, because a page that had happened to default to one of those two configurations would have shown paired and unpaired agreeing perfectly and concluded that pairing is a formality.

The second one is in the fold module and it is the one that nearly did happen. The correction factor is c = (1+(k−1)ρ)/(1−ρ), which is exactly 1 at ρ = 0. With independent folds the corrected test and the naive test are not close, they are the same statistic, agreeing to 0e+0 on every replication. A default of ρ = 0 would have produced a module in which a correction provably does nothing. The default is 0.5 and the ρ = 0 row is kept in the table as the control.

10. The folds are not independent and it is not a small effect

k-fold cross-validation trains on overlapping data, so the per-fold differences are correlated. Model that as equicorrelated normals with correlation ρ and the naive paired t-statistic is exactly √c times a genuine t(k−1) variate. The proof is three lines and it is the reason this module has an exact column: for an exchangeable normal vector the sample mean and the sample variance are still independent, the mean has variance σ²(1+(k−1)ρ)/k, and (k−1)s²/(σ²(1−ρ)) is still χ²(k−1). So the type I error is 2(1 − Fk−1(tcrit/√c)) in closed form.

At k = 10 and ρ = 0.5 that is 51.24%. A test advertised at 5% rejects a true null half the time, and dividing the statistic by √c puts it back to 5% on the nose. The direction of the k dependence is the part worth carrying around: at fixed ρ, more folds is worse — 16.44% at k = 3, 51.24% at k = 10, 65.30% at k = 20 — because extra folds add almost nothing to the effective sample size while the t-table happily narrows the critical value as if they had. Ten-fold cross-validation is not a safer significance test than three-fold; it is a more confident one.

11. The interval nobody checks

Reporting “+3.0 points ± 1.9” requires a standard error, and the one people reach for treats the two accuracies as independent proportions. On paired data that interval is too wide by the covariance term of section 8, and the over-coverage is not cosmetic: exactly 100.0000% at the middle world against a nominal 95%, with an interval 2.90× the width it should be. An interval that never misses is not a safe interval, it is a wasted experiment — it is the same information as the 6.7× item count, seen from the other end. The paired interval itself is not perfect either: its exact coverage is 94.73%, slightly under nominal, and unlike the naive one that number is identical in every world.

12. The seed guard, for the third time

Day 69 measured a 99.2% coverage that turned out to be an arithmetic seed stride feeding Box–Muller. Day 70 re-measured it for a uniform-only sampler and found nothing; Day 71 re-measured it against an exact target and again found nothing. This page has both kinds of sampler — a uniform categorical draw for the test items and a Box–Muller draw for the folds — and both have exact targets, so the check is run a third time on both. Both streams sit inside the sampling error of the exact answer in both modules, and the standard errors are printed so the reader can see what that is worth. An inherited caveat that fails to reproduce three times running, on four sampler-stream combinations, is a caveat about Day 69's particular arrangement and not about the generator.

13. Things I got wrong

The sawtooth in section 5, which was going to be the headline and turned out to average away; and the direction in section 7, which I had backwards until the sweep printed it. Two more. The first draft of the identity audit passed the world into the sampler and compared the resulting datasets, which is a weaker claim and also nearly a tautology; splitting the draw into drawDiscordant and paintWorld made the invariance structural rather than measured, and the measurement is still there because a structural argument with no count attached is how a bug hides. The second: the exact sample-size search was originally a linear scan calling a full power computation per n, which is O(n²) per call and took 77 seconds for one sweep of the MEASURE tab. The fix is that P(reject | d) does not depend on n at all, so one table serves every sample size — 240 milliseconds, and the same rearrangement is what makes the monotonicity scan over three thousand sizes affordable.

14. What this is not

One comparison. Two models, one test set, one hypothesis. Comparing twenty models on the same benchmark is a multiplicity problem and that was Day 64; stopping when the p-value looks good was Day 63. Nothing here is sequential and nothing here is corrected for a family.

The item-level half also assumes the test items are independent draws from the population you care about, which is exactly the assumption days 66 to 71 spent six days undermining, and the fold-level half models the correlation between folds as a single number ρ that it does not estimate. A real k-fold comparison has to estimate it, and the classic estimator — Nadeau and Bengio's 1/k + ntest/ntrain — is a plug-in for exactly this c. What the page shows is the size of the hole, not how to fill it from data.

🔨 How it is built

1. A 2×2 table, so the truth is a sum

The generator is four probabilities that sum to one. That single choice removes every integral from the item-level half of the page: power, size, the achieved level, the required sample size and the coverage of both intervals are finite sums over binomial and trinomial laws, computed in exact arithmetic. The Monte Carlo columns exist only as a check on those sums, are labelled as such, and carry a standard error. The fold half is a Gaussian model rather than a discrete one, and it earns its exact column a different way — the equicorrelated t distribution is √c times a standard one, so the type I error is a t CDF evaluation.

2. The sampler is not given a world

drawDiscordant(p10, p01, n, seed) takes four arguments and none of them says how accurate the models are. It returns a per-row mark in {0, 1, 2} and the two counts, which is the whole of what the test sees. A second function, paintWorld, fills in the rows marked 0 from a separate stream. The invariance is therefore structural — there is nowhere to put a world — and the page counts it anyway: 25 seeds × 600 rows × 4 world pairs, comparing the mark of every row plus the two counts plus the exact p-value, and printing the accuracy spread next to the zero so the reader can see that the worlds really are different.

3. P(reject | d) does not depend on n

This is the whole performance story. The conditional rejection probability at d disagreements is a function of d, θ and α only, so one table computed once serves every sample size, and the power at n becomes a short dot product against a windowed binomial — only the terms that carry mass, found by walking outwards from the mode until the ratio test drops below 1e-17 of the peak. An exact sample-size search over forty thousand candidates is then a few hundred milliseconds instead of a minute and a half, and scanning three thousand consecutive sizes for a dip in the power curve becomes something the page can do on every slider drag.

4. Two routes to the exact power

powerCond: sum_d Bin(d; n, delta) * sum_{i: min(i,d-i) <= c(d)} Bin(i; d, theta)
powerTri : sum_{i,j} n!/(i! j! (n-i-j)!) p10^i p01^j (1-delta)^(n-i-j) over the same region

Algebraically the same identity; numerically two different programs. One is a pair of nested recurrences in log space, the other is a flat double loop over log-gammas with no conditioning anywhere in it. They agree to about 3e-14 at n = 600, which is summation order. The verifier adds a third route from a different direction entirely — a forward simulator that draws two model outputs per item from the joint law and runs the test on the counts it observes — and a fourth that computes the same conditional binomial tails from a multiplicative recurrence rather than the page's log-gamma.

5. The rejection region, twice

The test is defined two ways in the engine and both are used: as a p-value (mcnemarP, the probability of a split at least this lopsided) compared to α, and as a critical count (critOf, the largest c whose two tails still fit under α) compared to min(n₁₀, n₀₁). The exact power uses the second because it is cheap; the simulation is run through both and the two rejection counts must be identical, which is a real check rather than a formality — an off-by-one in the ladder shows up here and nowhere else.

6. Where the tolerance comes from

Row marks and counts are integers, so the identity audit uses !== and expects exact zeros. Algebra does not get that treatment. The variance identity is a difference of products of numbers near one, so it is asserted at 1e-15 and measured at 1.1e-16; the two power routes are asserted at 1e-11 and measured at 3e-14; anything that passes through the normal CDF is asserted at 1e-6, because the tail rational in use is Numerical Recipes' erfcc, which bounds its fractional error at 1.2e-7 and says nothing whatever about absolute error — erfc(0) reads 1.00000003, not 1, and an assertion of 1e-15 there would be testing a promise the routine never made. Monte Carlo comparisons are asserted in units of their own standard error, never in absolute points. The windowed binomial is compared to the full vector at 1e-13 rather than 1e-15, and the reason is worth knowing: the full vector walks up from (1−p)n accumulating n logarithms and drifts by about 1e-14 at n = 400, while the window is anchored at the mode by a single log-gamma and walks outwards on exact ratios. The tolerance is set by the less accurate of the two, and it is not the new one.

The verifier learned the same lesson twice while being written, both times about its own oracles rather than the engine. Its reference erfc started life as 1 − Φ(x√2), which at x = 5 subtracts two numbers agreeing to twelve digits and keeps the rounding — a 4e-3 fractional error in the reference, blamed on the engine until the tail was integrated directly. And its first fold-level assertions were hand-typed constants carried over from a scratch run, which is only ever as good as that run; they are now an independent chain — a t CDF built as the ratio of two numerical integrals of the unnormalised density, needing no gamma function at all, and an inflation factor re-derived by summing the covariance matrix. A magic constant cannot tell you which side is wrong. An oracle can.

7. The t distribution, from scratch

The fold module needs Fν and its inverse for integer ν, which is the regularized incomplete beta: F(t) = 1 − ½ Ix(ν/2, ½) with x = ν/(ν+t²). That is a Lentz continued fraction and a log-gamma, forty lines, and the inverse is bisection on it. The verifier checks it by Simpson-integrating the t density directly, by the closed form at ν = 1 (Cauchy, where F(1) = ¾ exactly) and by convergence to the normal CDF at large ν.

8. Coverage against an exact number

The naive interval's width depends on all three free counts, so its exact coverage is a genuine triple sum: over the number of disagreements, over how they split, and over how many of the remaining rows both models got right. Windowing keeps it to about half a second for five worlds. It is worth the trouble — the answer at the middle world is 1.000000, and a simulation would have reported 1.0000 and left the reader unable to tell “never misses” from “missed twice in 2,000”.

9. The seed guard, run against known answers

Two samplers, two seed streams, two exact targets, one table. The multiplicative hash and the arithmetic stride that produced Day 69's artefact are both run through the uniform categorical sampler against the exact power, and through the Box–Muller fold sampler against the exact type I error. All four numbers are printed with the standard error beside them rather than summarised as a verdict.

10. Verification

The block between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END is DOM-free and is extracted by regex and run under Node against assertions written independently of it: xorshift32 in place of mulberry32 and the sine branch of Box–Muller where the page uses cosine; a normal CDF by Simpson integration against the page's rational tail; binomial probabilities by a multiplicative recurrence against the page's log-gamma; the t CDF by Simpson integration of its density against the page's continued fraction; the achieved level in exact BigInt integer arithmetic, for every p-value up to d = 30; the joint law of the two discordant counts by dynamic programming over items — no factorials, no logarithms and no conditioning anywhere in it; and — the one that matters — a forward simulator that draws, for each item, which of the four cells it fell into, then reconstructs the counts and runs the test. The page never draws an item that way; it starts from the two discordant probabilities. If the conditioning argument were wrong, the forward simulator's rejection rate would not match the exact power, and that is the check. The same simulator recovers the equicorrelated fold structure from its one-factor definition, which is the latent quantity the fold module only reasons about. It comes to 2,408 assertions in about nineteen seconds.
