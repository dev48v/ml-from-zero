Sixty-nine days of this series have treated y as the thing itself; it is a value somebody typed, a status a system inferred, a chart a clinician read. Write the two flip rates ρ₀ = P(ŷ=1 | y=0) and ρ₁ = P(ŷ=0 | y=1) and the observed law becomes affine in the truth: q = ρ₀ + (1−ρ₀−ρ₁)η. The feature is discrete — K cells with mass mk and an observed positive rate qk — so every population quantity on this page is a finite sum with no integration anywhere, and the ground truth is exact rather than simulated. The observed law is fixed: the sampler is handed (m, q) and nothing else. A world is a pair of flip rates (ρ₀, ρ₁), which forces ηk = (qk − ρ₀)/δ with δ = 1 − ρ₀ − ρ₁. Admissible worlds are exactly those where every ηk stays in [0,1]: ρ₀ ≤ 0.250 and ρ₁ ≤ 0.250. Coverage is over 400 replications; everything else is averaged over 25 seeds unless the row says otherwise.

The identified set does not move when the scores do

Same cells, same masses, same ordering — only how far the observed probabilities reach changes. A₀ is the best accuracy anybody can measure; Amax is the largest true Bayes accuracy any admissible world allows. I expected both to move together. They do not, and the reason is one line of algebra: A₀ = qlo + range·G while 1 − 2qlo = range exactly, so the range cancels and Amax = G — where G is what this same law would have measured with its scores stretched across the whole of [0,1].

The anchor-point estimator, and the direction of its error

ρ₀ ≤ min q is a bound, so the estimator everyone reaches for is the smallest fitted score — the anchor point. It is consistent. The question the page measures instead of assuming is which way it is wrong in a finite sample, because a minimum over K noisy rates is not the minimum of K true ones, and the answer decides whether the reported identified set is conservative or flattering.

Ranking two models: symmetric noise is safe, asymmetric noise is not

The affine identity, derived by substituting q = ρ₀ + δη: accnoisy(f) = ρ₀P(f=1) + ρ₁P(f=0) + δ·accclean(f). When ρ₀ = ρ₁ the first two terms collapse to a constant and measured accuracy is a strictly increasing function of true accuracy, so the number of inverted pairs is exactly 0 — a structural claim, not a small count that happened to come out low. When they differ, the surviving term is proportional to the classifier's predicted positive rate, which is not a measure of quality at all. The Bayes threshold moves, and what that costs

η > ½ is q > ρ₀ + δ/2 = (1 + ρ₀ − ρ₁)/2. Symmetric noise leaves the optimal cut at 0.5 exactly, which is why "train on noisy labels, threshold at a half" is not merely tolerable but optimal there. Asymmetric noise moves it, and the price is bounded by what lives between the two cuts — closed form ∑ mk|1 − 2ηk| over the cells that switch, checked against the measured difference in the next-to-last column.

The correction, and the fact that its two prices are one number

Told the true flip rates, you can build a loss on the recorded label whose expectation is the loss on the real one — a 2×2 solve, done here with Cramer's rule. For 0/1 loss it comes out as : agreeing with the recorded label earns a negative loss. Each branch has range exactly 1/δ, so the variance inflation is exactly 1/δ² — and dA/dρ divided by (2A₀−1) is 1/δ² as well. The cost of knowing ρ and the cost of getting it slightly wrong are the same quantity.

In-page self-test

The label is a recording too

Sixty-nine days of this series have taken y at face value. Day 69 finally admitted that a predictor is a recording of something and not the thing; this page does the same to the target, which is the column everything else is scored against. A fraud flag set by a rules engine that has both kinds of error. A diagnosis code entered under time pressure. "Churned", defined by a 30-day window that some customers straddle. A crowd worker's answer at 94% agreement. A returns label derived from a status field that a warehouse occasionally sets late.

The standard model of that is two numbers. Write y for the true label, ŷ for the one in your table, and

ρ₀ = P(ŷ = 1 | y = 0)     a clean case recorded as positive
ρ₁ = P(ŷ = 0 | y = 1)     a real case recorded as negative
δ  = 1 − ρ₀ − ρ₁      how much signal survives

It is called class-conditional noise: the flip rate may depend on the class but not on x within a class. Let η(x) = P(y = 1 | x) be the truth and q(x) = P(ŷ = 1 | x) be what your data can show you. Condition on the true label and add up the two ways a recorded 1 can arise:

q = (1 − ρ₁)η + ρ₀(1 − η)
  = ρ₀ + (1 − ρ₀ − ρ₁)η
  = ρ₀ + δη

The observed law is affine in the truth. That single line is responsible for everything on the MEASURE tab, and it cuts both ways: it makes the problem tractable, and it makes it unidentifiable.

The identified set, derived

Invert it. η = (q − ρ₀)/δ. Now fix the observed law — the masses mk and the observed rates qk — and ask which worlds could have produced it. The only requirement is that η is a probability:

ηk ≥ 0  for all k   ⇔   ρ₀ ≤ mink qk
ηk ≤ 1  for all k   ⇔   ρ₁ ≤ 1 − maxk qk

That is the whole thing. The identified set for the flip rates is a rectangle whose sides are exactly the distance from your model's score range to the ends of the unit interval. It is the same shape of argument as Day 69's Frisch bound — a variance could not be negative there, a probability cannot leave [0,1] here — and it has the same uncomfortable direction. A model whose predicted probabilities actually reach 0 and 1 somewhere pins the flip rates to zero: those are the anchor points, and their existence is the assumption under which the literature's noise-rate estimators are identified. A model whose scores all sit between 0.4 and 0.6 — an entirely ordinary model, the kind you would call "not very separable" and move on from — leaves them almost completely free.

So the sharper reading is not "label noise adds error". It is: the amount of label noise you could be suffering from is bounded by how confident your model is, which is not evidence about the labels at all.

What a measured accuracy is measuring

Take any classifier f. Its accuracy against the recorded labels — the number on the dashboard — and its accuracy against the truth are related by substituting the affine law into the definition. For the cells where f = 1 the contribution is q = ρ₀ + δη, and for the cells where f = 0 it is 1 − q = ρ₁ + δ(1 − η), so

accnoisy(f) = ρ₀·P(f=1) + ρ₁·P(f=0) + δ·accclean(f)

Two consequences, and they are opposites.

Symmetric noise is benign for ranking. If ρ₀ = ρ₁ = ρ then P(f=1) + P(f=0) = 1 collapses the first two terms to the constant ρ, and

accnoisy = ρ + (1 − 2ρ)·accclean

which is strictly increasing in accclean whenever ρ < ½. Every pair of classifiers keeps its order, ties stay ties, the argmax is unchanged, and the Bayes threshold stays at 0.5 exactly. Model selection on noisy labels is correct under symmetric noise. The page checks this over all 2K classifiers rather than asserting it, and the inversion count is 0 because it cannot be anything else.

Asymmetric noise is not. The surviving term is (ρ₀ − ρ₁)·P(f=1), and the predicted positive rate is not a measure of quality. Subtracting the identity for two classifiers,

Δaccnoisy = δ·Δaccclean + (ρ₀ − ρ₁)·ΔP(f=1)

so a classifier can buy measured accuracy purely by predicting positive more often, and it inverts the ranking whenever the second term outweighs the first. The page finds the worst such pair by brute force and confirms the closed form reproduces its measured gap to 1e-16.

Deconvolution, and the range that cancels

Rearranging the symmetric case gives the map everybody wants:

accclean = (accnoisy − ρ) / (1 − 2ρ)

Apply it to A₀, the best accuracy anyone can measure, and you get the true Bayes accuracy of the world with flip rate ρ. Differentiate:

dA/dρ = (2A₀ − 1) / (1 − 2ρ)²   > 0  whenever A₀ > ½

The inferred truth is increasing in the assumed noise rate, so the largest true accuracy consistent with your data sits at the corner ρ = ρmax. A grid search over the whole admissible rectangle — not just the symmetric diagonal — confirms the maximum is at that corner, which is the second independent route to the same number.

Now the part I did not see coming. On this page's law, qk = qlo + range·gk with gk the rank position in [0,1], and since max(q, 1−q) = qlo + range·max(g, 1−g) we get A₀ = qlo + range·G where G = ∑ mkmax(gk, 1−gk). The bound is ρmax = qlo and 1 − 2qlo = range, so

Amax = (A₀ − qlo)/(1 − 2qlo) = (range·G)/range = G

The range cancels exactly. The top of the identified set does not depend on how far your scores reach at all — it is a property of the cell ordering and its masses, and it equals what this same law would have measured with its scores stretched over the whole of [0,1]. Which, once seen, is obvious: the un-squeezed version of the observed law is the extreme admissible world. Improving separation does not raise the ceiling on what could be true; it lowers the ceiling on what could be wrong.

Why every diagnostic is blind

The sampler on this page is sampleObserved(n, law, seed). The law is (m, q). There is no ρ to pass, so two worlds do not merely produce similar data — they produce the identical arrays. Held-out accuracy, log-loss, Brier, AUC, the calibration curve, cross-validation, a learning curve, a bootstrap of any of them: all are functions of those arrays, so all are bit-identical. That is not a limitation of the diagnostics. Every one of them is doing its job correctly on the labels it was given.

Calibration deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the diagnostic that sounds like it should catch this. A model fitted on ŷ is calibrated against ŷ, and the gap to the truth is available in closed form:

q − η = (ρ₀ − q(ρ₀ + ρ₁)) / δ
        = ρ(1 − 2q)/(1 − 2ρ)      when ρ₀ = ρ₁ = ρ

which is 0 at q = ½ and changes sign either side, so noise pulls every probability towards a half and a perfectly flat reliability diagram is compatible with being wrong by a fifth on both tails. Day 44 built calibration; this page is where it runs out of road, and the reason is that it is calibration against the recorded label.

The correction, and its two prices

If someone tells you (ρ₀, ρ₁), you can fix the loss instead of the labels. Ask for a function L of the recorded label whose conditional expectation is the real loss, and write the two conditions down:

(1−ρ₁)L₁ + ρ₁L₀     = l(t, 1)        taking E[· | y = 1]
ρ₀L₁     + (1−ρ₀)L₀ = l(t, 0)        taking E[· | y = 0]

determinant = (1−ρ₀)(1−ρ₁) − ρ₀ρ₁ = 1 − ρ₀ − ρ₁ = δ

Cramer's rule finishes it, and the determinant tells you where the problem is before you have even substituted a loss: everything is divided by δ, which goes to zero as the noise approaches a coin flip. For 0/1 loss the four values are

predict 1:   on ŷ=1  −ρ₁/δ        on ŷ=0  (1−ρ₁)/δ
predict 0:   on ŷ=1  (1−ρ₀)/δ   on ŷ=0  −ρ₀/δ

Agreeing with the recorded label earns you a negative loss. Each branch has range (1−ρ)/δ + ρ/δ = 1/δ exactly, against a range of 1 for the uncorrected loss, so the standard error is inflated by exactly 1/δ and you need 1/δ² times as many rows to match the naive estimator's precision. It is also unbounded below, so the estimate of an error rate can be negative, and the page counts how often against a closed-form prediction.

And that 1/δ² is the same number as dA/dρ divided by (2A₀ − 1). The price of knowing the flip rates and the price of mis-stating them are one quantity, which is a tidier way to say the thing everybody says loosely: near a coin flip, nothing helps.

What to actually do

The honest summary is that no analysis of (x, ŷ) can choose a world, so the choice has to come from outside the data:

Audit a subsample against a better instrument. A few hundred rows relabelled by adjudication estimates ρ₀ and ρ₁ directly and turns an unidentified parameter into a measurement — the same move Day 69 needed a second recording of a predictor for. It is the only one of these that is not an assumption.

Argue for an anchor point. If some region of feature space is certainly negative, the flip rate is pinned there. This is an assumption, but it is a checkable, arguable, domain one rather than a statistical convenience.

Prefer the ranking questions. AUC and any threshold-free comparison survive symmetric noise cleanly, and under asymmetric noise the damage is proportional to (ρ₀ − ρ₁) times a difference in positive rates — which you can at least see, because predicted positive rates are observable.

Report the set. "72.9% measured, and anywhere from 72.9% to 95.7% true depending on a flip rate this data cannot bound below 0.25" is a sentence a stakeholder can act on. A single number with a confidence interval on it is not, because the interval is around the wrong quantity and shrinking it makes that worse.

🔨 How it is built

1. A discrete feature, so the ground truth is a sum

lawOf(cfg)   -> { m, q, cdf, qLo, qHi, Q, A0, G, rho0Max, rho1Max, Amax, setWidth }
worldOf(law, rho0, rho1) -> { eta, delta, pi1, bayesClean, fStar, tStar, calGap }

The feature takes K values, so a "population quantity" is a weighted sum over K cells and there is no numerical integration anywhere on the page. The Bayes accuracy, the true prevalence, the accuracy of any classifier in any world, the calibration gap — all exact to the last bit at the default settings, which means every measured number has something exact to be compared against rather than a second simulation.

2. The sampler is not given a world

sampleObserved(n, law, seed)   // law is (m, q). There is no rho parameter to pass.

This is the same device as Day 68 and Day 69 and it is the load-bearing part of the page. The claim "these worlds share their observed data" is not a numerical coincidence checked afterwards; it is the only thing that could have happened, because the function that produces the data has no argument that could carry the difference. The audit still re-runs all nine diagnostics once per world and compares them with !==, on the grounds that the one way this could break — ρ leaking into the sampler during some later edit — is exactly the sort of thing an assertion should be standing guard over.

The reverse direction is checked too: every world rebuilds the observed law it came from, ρ₀ + δηk against qk, and lands at 5.6e-17. Not zero, and for the reason Day 68 and Day 69 both ran into: ρ₀ + δ((q − ρ₀)/δ) is an exact algebraic identity that binary64 does not honour bit for bit. The tolerance is 1e-15 and it is documented rather than quietly widened.

3. Where === is right

accClean(law, cleanWorld, f) === accNoisy(law, f)      // asserted with ===, 400/400 reps

In the clean world ηk = (qk − 0)/1, and subtracting zero and dividing by one are exact in binary64, so the two functions execute the identical sequence of operations on identical inputs. This one is a genuine bit-equality and the page asserts it as one. Knowing which identities survive === and which do not is the difference between a tolerance that means something and a tolerance that hides a bug: the three that need one are the law rebuild, the affine identity and the deconvolution, all at 2–3e-16 across 1,536 and 1,280 checked combinations.

4. The whole hypothesis space, not a sample of it

allClassifiers(law)   // 2^K of them for K <= 12, thresholds above that

The inversion count is over every pair from all 2K classifiers — 32,640 pairs at K = 8 — and not a sampled subset, because the claim about symmetric noise is that the count is exactly zero and a sampled zero would prove nothing. The asymmetric count is then a real census rather than an estimate, and the worst inversion it finds is checked against the closed form δΔaccclean + (ρ₀−ρ₁)ΔP(f{=}1), which reproduces its measured gap to 8e-17.

5. Two routes to the maximum

closed form:  Amax = (A0 - rhoMax)/(1 - 2*rhoMax)        along the symmetric diagonal
brute force:  121 x 121 grid over the admissible rectangle, eta checked cell by cell

The derivation only shows the maximum is at a corner of the symmetric slice. The grid searches the whole rectangle, rejects any point where some ηk leaves [0,1], and reports where it found the best and worst Bayes accuracy: 14,641 points, 0 rejected, maximum at (ρmax, ρmax), and it agrees with the closed form to 0 — exactly, not to a tolerance. The invariance Amax = G is checked at six score ranges and holds to 2.2e-16, which is how I found it: the column simply refused to move.

6. The interval is computed once and scored five times

for each replication:
    train = sampleObserved(n, law, repSeed(11, r))       // world-free
    test  = sampleObserved(nTest, law, repSeed(12, r))   // world-free
    f     = fitCells(train).f                            // threshold qHat at 0.5
    ci    = waldCi(accOnSample(test, f), nTest)           // ONE interval
    for each world:  truth = accClean(law, world, f);  did ci cover it?

Five coverages read off the same intervals, so "95% here and 0% there" cannot be an artefact of five slightly different estimators. Wilson re-scores the same replications for the same reason at the interval level: at n = 2000 it moves the clean-world coverage by about a tenth of a point and leaves the 0.0% exactly where it was, which is the answer to "is this just Wald undercovering".

7. The seed guard, and what it found this time

hashed:  repSeed(tag, r) = (imul(r+1, 2654435761) ^ imul(tag+1, 0x9E3779B1)) >>> 0
stride:  44000 + s*131 + r*7907          // the stream that broke Day 69

Day 69 measured 99.2% coverage where the truth was 95.2%, purely because an arithmetic progression of mulberry32 seeds is not a random seed stream. The rule that came out of it was to hash, and to assert the hash. So this page hashes — and then runs the broken stride alongside it and prints both, because the honest version of "learn the lesson" is to re-measure rather than to assume the same failure repeats. It does not: this sampler is Bernoulli rather than Gaussian, both streams land on 94.0% / 94.0%, and the artefact is specific to Box–Muller consuming pairs of correlated uniforms. The guard stays in anyway, now as a measurement instead of a warning.

8. Averaging, and the number I nearly shipped

At my first choice of 400 replications the clean-world coverage came back 93.3%, which is 1.5 standard errors low and reads like a mild finding about Wald intervals. It is not — at 1,000 replications it is 95.00% and at 4,000 it is 94.83%. The standard error of a coverage estimate over R replications is √(0.95·0.05/R), which is 1.1 points at R = 400 and 0.7 at R = 1,000, so the default moved up and the slider goes to 2,400 if you want to watch it settle. The auxiliary studies keep their own budgets rather than scaling with it, because they are checks and not the headline — and the correction table needs almost none, since for symmetric ρ the corrected loss is the exact affine map (raw − ρ)/δ of the uncorrected one, which makes the sd ratio 1/δ with no Monte Carlo error in it at all. Every level claim is averaged over the count shown on its row, because Day 66 lost an effect inside a standard error that was larger than it.

10. Things I got wrong

I expected the identified set to shrink from both ends as separation improved. It shrinks from below only. Amax is pinned at 0.957142857142857 across every score range from 0.20 to 0.98, to within 2.2e-16, and the algebra says it has to be: the range cancels. The table was built to show two columns moving together and instead showed one of them refusing to move, which is a better result than the one I was looking for.

I expected the threshold shift to be the expensive failure. At the default it costs 0.71 accuracy points, because the mass is at the ends and there is nothing between the two cuts to move. It is the ranking inversion that is expensive: a pair whose measured gap is 0.18 points and whose true gap is 32.3, in the same asymmetric world. The threshold sweep is on the page because the small number needed explaining rather than hiding, and the closed form ∑mk|1−2ηk| over the switching cells reproduces it exactly at every mass shape.

My first negative-risk count was 0 out of 200 and I read it as "does not happen". The normal approximation says 1.6% at 2,000 test rows, so 0 of 200 replications is the expected outcome and not evidence of anything. Adding the predicted column turned a null result into a real one: at 200 test rows it is 23.0% measured against 24.8% predicted, and an error rate below zero is a thing that happens to roughly one run in four.
