IN-PAGE SELF-TEST

Three things called "drift"

A deployed model degrades and somebody says the data drifted. That word covers three different factorisations of the joint distribution p(x, y), and which one moved determines whether you can fix it, how, and whether you can even see it.

  • Covariate shift. p(x) changes, p(y|x) is unchanged. Your users got younger; the relationship between age and churn did not move.
  • Label shift (prior shift). p(y) changes, p(x|y) is unchanged. Disease prevalence tripled; the symptoms of the disease did not change.
  • Concept drift. p(y|x) changes. The same customer, with the same features, now behaves differently. A competitor launched; a policy changed; fraudsters adapted.

These are not degrees of the same problem. They have different fixes, and one of them is not fixable from unlabelled data at all.

The uncomfortable part about covariate shift

The textbook line is that covariate shift breaks your model. It usually does not.

If your model class contains the truth, ordinary least squares is consistent for the true parameters under any p(x) with full support. Shift the features anywhere you like: the same coefficients are still being estimated, still without bias. What shifting costs you is variance, because you are now extrapolating — and that cost is O(1/n) and vanishes with data.

Covariate shift only produces bias when the model is misspecified. Then "the best line" stops being a single thing: the line that minimises squared error under ptrain is a different line from the one that minimises it under ptest, because least squares weights errors by where the data is. Least squares converges to the first one. The gap between them is O(1) and does not shrink with n, ever.

So the harm from covariate shift is the product of two things — how far the features moved, and how wrong your model was — and a drift detector measures exactly one of them.

Why the monitor cannot help you

Every standard drift detector — KS test, PSI, a domain classifier's AUC, MMD — is a function of the feature values alone. Feed it two datasets with the same features and it returns the same number, whatever the labels are doing. That is not a limitation of tuning. It is what the function's arguments are.

The MEASURE tab makes that concrete rather than rhetorical. Two rows of the first table are handed the identical arrays of x, so their KS, AUC and PSI agree to the last bit — and their true excess risk differs by two and a half orders of magnitude. Two other rows are also handed identical x, and one of them has a catastrophic p(y|x) change that no detector reports at all.

The practical reading is not "monitoring is useless". It is that a feature-drift alarm is a prompt to go and measure something else, and a quiet monitor is not evidence that anything is fine.

Importance weighting, and its bill

The classical repair for covariate shift is to reweight the training loss by the density ratio:

w(x) = p_test(x) / p_train(x)
minimise  Σ w(x_i) · (y_i − f(x_i))²

This works because the weighted training risk is an unbiased estimate of the test risk, so the fit now targets the test domain's best line instead of the training domain's. On this page the ratio is available exactly — both densities are known Gaussians — so what is being measured is the method, not a density-estimation error on top of it.

Two things follow, and both are measured:

  • When the model is well specified, the two targets are the same line. Weighting removes a bias that was not there and adds variance that is. It makes the model strictly worse, and the page shows by how much.
  • When the model is misspecified, weighting removes a genuine O(1) bias and is worth several multiples of its variance cost.

The variance cost has a name. Kish's effective sample size, (Σw)²/Σw², is roughly how many unweighted rows your weighted rows are worth. For a pure mean shift of Δ with equal spreads the population value is exactly exp(−Δ²/σ²) — so a 2-sigma shift leaves under 2% of your data doing the work.

And the diagnostic is trustworthy right up until the moment it matters, then fails in one direction. While the true ESS is comfortable the measured value is essentially exact — 77.84% against a true 77.88% at a half-sigma shift. Once the weights turn heavy-tailed the enormous ones live where the training density is tiny, so a finite sample simply never draws them and the number comes back optimistic: 1.5× too high at 1.5 sigma, 3.6× at 2 sigma, 160× at 3 sigma. Nor does more data rescue it quickly — at a 2-sigma shift the sample says 6.6% at n = 800, 3.0% at n = 20,000 and is still saying 2.0% at n = 500,000, against a true 1.8%. A green ESS is only evidence when it is very green.

There is also a hard boundary. E[w²] is finite only when σtest² < 2σtrain². Past that the weights have infinite variance and importance weighting has no valid standard error at all — while the empirical ESS still cheerfully reports a comfortable-looking percentage.

Label shift is the friendly one

If p(x|y) is unchanged and only p(y) moved, you can recover the new prior from unlabelled test data. The trick is that a classifier's true and false positive rates are properties of p(x|y), so label shift leaves them alone:

P_test(Ŷ = 1) = π·TPR + (1 − π)·FPR
       Ŷ  →   π̂ = (q̂ − FPR) / (TPR − FPR)

Measure the prediction rate on unlabelled test features, use TPR and FPR from your labelled training data, and solve. That is black-box shift estimation in its two-class form. Then move the decision threshold to the Bayes threshold for the new prior — for two Gaussians separated by 2d that is −logit(π)/(2d) — and the accuracy comes back. No test labels were used anywhere.

Concept drift is the one that ends the story

If p(y|x) changed, no reweighting of x can help, and this is not an empirical claim but an arithmetic one. The weights are a function of x only. If p(x) did not move, every weight is exactly 1 and the "corrected" fit is bit-for-bit the uncorrected fit. The page asserts that rather than illustrating it.

Concept drift is detectable only with labels, which arrive late or not at all — and the honest response is to build the labelling loop rather than to buy a better feature monitor.

The honest caveat about this page

One feature, Gaussian everything, a quadratic truth and a linear model. That choice is what makes the true best predictor and the true risk available in closed form, so nothing here is compared against another estimate. The structural claims — weights identically 1 when p(x) is unchanged, detectors invariant to labels, the two best-fit targets coinciding exactly when the model is correct — do not depend on any of it. The specific magnitudes do.

🔨 How it is built

1. A generator that separates p(x) from p(y|x)

domain  = { mu, sd }      // this IS p(x)
concept = { a, b, c }     // this IS p(y|x):  y = a + b·x + c·x² + ε

Every scenario is a pair of (domain, concept) pairs. Covariate shift changes the domain and holds the concept fixed; concept drift changes the concept and holds the domain fixed. Because those are separate objects, the page can assert what each scenario is supposed to be instead of hoping the generator cooperated — and the x sampler takes its own seed stream, so changing the concept cannot perturb the features by even one bit.

2. The best linear predictor, in closed form

β₁* = b + 2cμ
β₀* = a + cσ² − cμ²

For x ~ N(μ, σ²) and a quadratic truth, Cov(x, x²) = 2μσ², which gives the population least-squares line directly. At c = 0 both formulas lose their μ and σ and collapse to (a, b) — the same line for every domain, which is exactly what "the model is well specified" means, and the page checks that identity to machine precision before using it.

3. The exact population risk of any line

E[(f(x) + ε − β₀ − β₁x)²]
  = τ² + A² + B²m₂ + c²m₄ + 2ABm₁ + 2Acm₂ + 2Bcm₃
      A = a − β₀,  B = b − β₁,  mₖ = E[xₖ] under the test domain

No test set is scored anywhere. A fitted line is handed to this expression and gets its true risk on the test domain, with the irreducible part subtracted off so the column reads zero for a perfect fit. That is the whole reason the numbers in the last column can be trusted while the numbers in the first three columns are noisy estimates.

4. The detectors, which never see y

ksStat(xTrain, xTest)              // max ECDF gap
psi(xTrain, xTest, 10)             // training deciles as bins
domainClassifierAUC(xTrain, xTest) // train-vs-test logistic, AUC on a held-out half

The domain classifier is fitted on half the pooled rows and scored on the other half, so its AUC sits at 0.5 under no shift rather than at a small in-sample optimism. All three take x only, which is the point of the page.

5. Importance weights that are exact, not estimated

w(x) = normPdf(x, test.mu, test.sd) / normPdf(x, train.mu, train.sd)

// and the population second moment, so ESS can be audited:
E[w²] = (σt / σe²√α)·exp(−(γ − β²/α)/2),  α = 2/σe² − 1/σt²
α ≤ 0  ⇒  the integral diverges: infinite weight variance

Estimating the ratio would have made this a page about density estimation. Given exactly, what remains is the method's own behaviour — including the fact that it makes a well-specified model worse.

6. Averaged over seeds, and the count is on the page

for (let s = 0; s < seeds; s++) { ... }   // default 25

Several effects here are smaller than one run's standard error. The ESS overstatement, the variance cost of weighting on a correct model, and the small extrapolation penalty from covariate shift all need averaging before they mean anything — reading any of them off a single seed produces a number with the wrong sign often enough to matter.

7. What the tests pin

assert(detector(cov, c=0) === detector(cov, c=0.6));  // EXACT equality, same arrays
assert(excess(cov, c=0.6) > 100 × excess(cov, c=0));
assert(max|w − 1| === 0) when p(x) is unchanged;        // weighting is a literal no-op
