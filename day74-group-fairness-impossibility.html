Measure it separately on two groups and a new question appears that has nothing to do with accuracy: which of the equalities you would like can hold at the same time? That turns out to be arithmetic rather than policy, and it is answerable exactly, so this page computes instead of arguing. The world here is a law with rational cell probabilities, not a sample: the class-conditional score distribution is identical in both groups, so the two ROC curves are the same curve and the AUC gap is exactly 0; the decision is a function of the score bin alone, so “fairness through unawareness” already holds for free; and the only difference between the groups is the base rate, 30% against 8%. At the payoff-optimal cut both groups get true-positive rate 0.7759 and false-positive rate 0.1719 — to the last bit — and yet 35.31% of group A is flagged against 22.02% of group B, a gap of exactly 0.1329, which is exactly (πA − πB) × J — the prevalence gap times Youden’s J. The whole disparity is one product, and the largest it can ever get is the total-variation distance between the two groups’ score distributions, 0.132880: blindness buys you that bound, not zero. A flag is also 2.34× as likely to be correct in group A (0.6592 against 0.2819), because FPR = odds(π) × (1−PPV)/PPV × (1−FNR) is an identity and it leaves you two of the three equalities, never three — which is not asserted here but proved by exhaustion: all 405,909 pairs of integer confusion matrices over 105 pairs of unequal margins contain 0 non-degenerate simultaneous solutions, while the equal-base-rate control contains 680. The world is a law, not a sample. A score bin v carries the exact binomial likelihood ratio P(S=v|Y=1) = C(m,v) tv / (1+t)m against P(S=v|Y=0) = C(m,v) / 2m with m = 10, so the log-likelihood ratio is exactly v ln t − m ln((1+t)/2), both normalisers are closed forms, and every rate on this page is a ratio of integers computed in BigInt rather than an estimate. one cut for everyone — the group-blind control

 

 

the relation that turns three equalities into two

Not quoted, derived: PPV = πs / (πs + (1−π)f), so (1−PPV)/PPV = (1−π)f / (πs), and multiplying by odds(π) s leaves f. That is one line, it holds for every confusion matrix with something flagged, and it means precision, recall and the false-positive rate are three numbers with two degrees of freedom once the base rate is fixed. The residual column is computed in exact rationals, so it is zero and not small.

 

what blindness actually buys

The largest flag-rate gap that any group-blind threshold rule can produce is not something to be estimated. Because P(S=v|A) − P(S=v|B) = (πA−πB)(p1[v] − p0[v]), summing the positive part gives the total-variation distance between the two groups’ score distributions — which is also exactly how strong a proxy for the group the score is. Three quantities, one number.

 

the gap as a function of how good the model is

The skill dial is t, the base of the likelihood ratio, and t = 1 is a model that knows nothing. The bound column is the exact maximum gap. The deployed columns are the gap at the cut a practitioner would actually pick. Watch the two columns disagree about the direction.

 

the accuracy-optimal rule is not group-blind

Flag v when π p1[v] > (1−π) p0[v], i.e. when the likelihood ratio clears (1−π)/π. Here that is a closed form in v, and the two groups’ crossing points differ by exactly (logit πA − logit πB) / ln t. So the rule that maximises accuracy reads the group attribute, and using one cut for everybody is already a fairness constraint with a price.

 

the repairs, all measured on the same file

Each row is what somebody does when told to fix one thing. The first row is the control — the payoff-optimal cut, applied identically, blind to the group in the strongest sense available. Every other row is scored against it. Δflag is how much of each group’s decision changed; Δrecall B is what the repair did to the smaller group’s true positives.

 

who moves — a choice the criterion does not make

Every repair above moved group B, because that is what the code was told to do. Nothing in any of the definitions says which group moves. Here is the other direction, on the same file — and sometimes the arithmetic does not offer it.

 

whether a repair is even possible is not monotone in skill

Predictive parity needs group B to reach group A’s precision, and group B’s precision has a ceiling: the calibrated probability of its top bin. Both the ceiling and the target move with t, and the cut is an integer, so feasibility flips more than once.

 

the twin: nothing above knows whether a flag is good news

Read a flag as a shortlist place and read it as extra scrutiny. Every number on this page is bit-identical under both readings — and each repair swaps the group it helps.

 

the same score does not mean the same thing

The calibrated probability in group g is πgp1[v] / (πgp1[v] + (1−πg)p0[v]), which in logit form is logit πg + v ln t − m ln((1+t)/2). Same slope in both groups, intercepts differing by exactly logit πA − logit πB: one curve, shifted sideways. The odds-ratio column is the same rational in every row, which is that statement.

So a probability has to be attached to the score somehow, and there are only three ways to do it. Two of them require the attribute.

 

calibration and balance cannot both hold either

For any calibrated score, π E[c|Y=1] + (1−π) E[c|Y=0] = E[c] = π, so E[c|Y=0] = odds(π) × (1 − E[c|Y=1]). One of the two conditional means determines the other. Equalise both across groups and the odds have to match — unless E[c|Y=1] = 1, which is perfect prediction. Derived, then checked in exact rationals.

 

unawareness, measured rather than assumed

The policy object on this page has exactly two fields and neither is the group. That is checked structurally, not asserted. Here is what it is worth.

 

the impossibility, proved by exhaustion

Every integer confusion matrix, for both groups, with equality tested by integer cross-multiplication so nothing is “within tolerance”. No sampling and no algebra — a count. Then the same count over every pair of unequal margins, and then the control that isolates the mechanism: equal base rates, where the impossibility has no reason to bite.

 

the price of equalised odds, when the curves differ

Above, both groups share one ROC curve, so equalised odds is reachable with a single cut. Reachable is not free: the unconstrained optimum uses two cuts. Now weaken the model in group B only and the curves come apart, so the constraint needs a point on both. Both curves are concave — the likelihood ratio is monotone in the bin — so the feasible set is the region under the lower one and randomising with “flag nobody” supplies the chords. Searched exactly over rationals.

in-page checks

Which equalities can hold at once?

Seventy-three days of this series measured a model on a dataset. Split the dataset by a column — any column that names a group of people — and measure separately, and a question appears that accuracy cannot answer. The two groups get different numbers. Which of those numbers should be made equal?

The honest answer is that you do not get to pick more than one, and the reason is not political. It is a line of algebra you can write in ten seconds.

1. A confusion matrix has fewer degrees of freedom than it looks

Fix a group’s base rate π. Pick a decision rule; it has a true-positive rate s and a false-positive rate f. Then the fraction flagged is πs + (1−π)f and precision is πs / (πs + (1−π)f). Everything else follows. In particular

(1 - PPV)/PPV = (1-pi)*f / (pi*s)
=> f = odds(pi) * (1-PPV)/PPV * s
     = odds(pi) * (1-PPV)/PPV * (1 - FNR)

which is the whole story. Precision, recall and the false-positive rate are three numbers with two degrees of freedom, and the third one is pinned by the base rate. So if two groups have different base rates and you equalise precision and recall, the false-positive rates come out in the ratio of the prevalence odds — not approximately, exactly. You can have two of the three. Never three.

The page does not ask you to take that on faith. It enumerates every integer confusion matrix for both groups and counts the simultaneous solutions. There are always a couple, and they are always the same two degenerate ones: flag only true positives (precision 1 in both groups, false-positive rate 0 in both) or flag only false positives (precision 0 in both). Strip those out and the count is 0, over hundreds of thousands of matrices and every pair of unequal margins. Then set the two base rates equal and the count is in the hundreds. That control is the point: the impossibility is a fact about unequal base rates, not about confusion matrices.

2. The disparity is one product

Apply a single threshold to everybody. Because the two groups share a score law given the label, they get the same s and the same f. The fraction flagged differs anyway:

sel_A - sel_B = (pi_A - pi_B) * (s - f)
             = (prevalence gap) * Youden's J

Two factors, and only one of them is about the model. The other is a fact about the world that no amount of engineering touches. Set J = 0 — a model that knows nothing — and the gap is exactly zero. That is the only group-blind rule with equal error rates and equal flag rates, and the page prints its accuracy so you can see what it costs.

The maximum over all thresholds has a name. It is the total-variation distance between the two groups’ score distributions, which is exactly the extent to which the score is a proxy for the group. So not looking at the attribute does not bound the disparity at zero; it bounds it at how visible the attribute is in the score. On this page that is 0.1329, and the score’s AUC for predicting the group is 0.58 — a weak proxy, doing all of the work.

3. 🔴 The claim this page was built around is false

Because the gap is (prevalence gap) × J, and J rises with skill, it looks like a theorem that a better model has a bigger gap. For the bound it is: over 320 skill steps across ten pairs of base rates and four bin counts, the bound never once decreases. For the rule you actually deploy it is not, and the reason is dull rather than deep. The accuracy-optimal cut is an integer; when it moves, the operating point can land in a lower-J region. Nine of the same 320 steps raise accuracy and shrink the deployed gap, the worst by 0.030 while accuracy improves by 0.005.

The claim survives as a statement about what is possible and dies as a statement about what happens. That distinction is not decoration: a team that ships a better model and watches the disparity fall has not disproved anything, and a team that watches it rise has not caused anything.

4. Group-blindness is already a constraint

The accuracy-maximising decision is to flag v when π p1[v] > (1−π) p0[v]. That compares the likelihood ratio to (1−π)/π, which is group-specific. In this world it is a closed form: the crossing point in bins is [ln((1−π)/π) + m ln((1+t)/2)] / ln t, and the two groups’ crossing points differ by exactly (logit πA − logit πB) / ln t.

So the Bayes rule reads the attribute, uses two different cuts, and violates equal opportunity by 28 points. Using one cut for everybody is a choice that costs accuracy, and on this page it costs 0.0054 — and the same number is the price of equalised odds when the two curves coincide, because a single cut is what equalised odds reduces to there. The neutral baseline is not neutral; it is one point on the trade-off with a price tag nobody prints.

5. What each repair actually does

Demographic parity, equal opportunity, equalised odds and predictive parity are all in the repairs table, solved exactly: because a randomised cut makes each rate a piecewise linear function of one rational parameter, the equality that comes back is an equality and not a tolerance. Then everything else is measured.

Demographic parity zeroes the flag gap, widens the precision gap from 0.377 to 0.462, opens TPR and FPR gaps that were exactly zero, and costs 0.035 accuracy. Over every configuration swept, all five of those hold every time.

Predictive parity is the one that reads best in a meeting — a flag should mean the same thing in both groups — and it is the one with the largest bill. It zeroes the precision gap by moving group B’s bar up until group B’s flag rate falls from 22.0% to 3.0% and its recall from 0.776 to 0.246: two thirds of that group’s true positives, dropped. It also raises population accuracy. A scoreboard would call this an improvement.

And it is not symmetric. Group A’s precision can never fall to group B’s, because the lowest precision any rule can have in a group is that group’s base rate, and group A’s base rate is above group B’s achieved precision. So the arithmetic offers exactly one direction. Which group pays is not a policy decision here; it has already been made, by the numbers, before anyone was asked.

Feasibility is not even monotone. Group B’s precision has a ceiling — the calibrated probability of its top bin — and both the ceiling and group A’s precision rise with skill while the cut jumps in integers. On one setting predictive parity is impossible, then possible, then impossible, then possible again as the model improves.

6. Nothing above knows whether a flag is good news

Read the flag as a shortlist place and every repair helps one group; read it as extra scrutiny and the same repair helps the other. Every number is bit-identical. The criteria are stated over confusion matrices, and a confusion matrix does not record whether the person wanted to be in the positive cell. This is the same shape as Day 68’s missing-data twins: two worlds agreeing on every observable and disagreeing on the answer, with the deciding quantity outside the data.

7. Calibration is not an escape hatch

A score has to be turned into a probability, and there are three ways. Calibrate on group A and ship that map: it is wrong on group B by 0.130, and the error is entirely one-sided — the bias equals the calibration error exactly, so it overstates risk for group B in every bin. Calibrate on the pooled data: now it is wrong on both groups, low on one and high on the other. Calibrate per group: correct everywhere, and it needs the attribute.

There is also a second impossibility hiding in calibration, and it needs no thresholds at all. For any calibrated score, πE[c|Y=1] + (1−π)E[c|Y=0] = E[c] = π, so the mean score among positives determines the mean score among negatives. Ask for both to be equal across groups and the odds ratio has to be 1, unless the mean score among positives is exactly 1 — perfect prediction. Equalise the first and the gap in the second is forced to 0.141 on this page. The page derives the identity and then checks it as an exact zero rather than citing it.

8. What this is not

This page is not an argument that any of these criteria is the right one, and it is not an argument that disparities do not matter. It is narrower and, I think, more useful: the four criteria are not four opinions about the same quantity, they are four different quantities, and the arithmetic that connects them is fixed. Choosing among them is a decision about who bears which error, it cannot be deferred to a metric, and it cannot be satisfied by leaving a column out of the model — on this page the column was never in the model and every gap is there anyway.

🔨 How it is built

1. A law, not a sample

The discipline of this run is that ground truth must be a closed form or an exact computation, never an estimate. So there is no dataset. There is a joint law over (group, label, score bin) whose cells are exact rationals, and every quantity on the page is a ratio of BigInts. A rational is {n, d} reduced by Euclid’s algorithm; addition, multiplication and comparison are the obvious four lines each; equality is a.n*b.d === b.n*a.d, so “these two are equal” is an integer fact.

The score law is chosen so that both normalisers are closed forms and the likelihood ratio is exactly geometric:

p1[v] = C(m,v) * t^v / (1+t)^m      sum = (1+t)^m / (1+t)^m = 1
p0[v] = C(m,v)       / 2^m          sum = 2^m / 2^m = 1
LR(v) = p1[v]/p0[v] = t^v * (2/(1+t))^m

which makes the score a calibrated logit with slope ln t and a group-specific intercept, and puts “a model with no skill” at t = 1 rather than at some tuned limit. Binomial coefficients come from a memoised Pascal triangle in BigInt; the powers from binary exponentiation.

2. Randomised cuts, so equality is exact

A deterministic threshold gives a finite set of operating points, and no two groups will have a matching one except by luck. So a policy is a pair (j, q): flag everything at or above bin j, plus a fraction q of the items sitting in bin j−1. Every rate is then piecewise linear in q and monotone in the cut, so each equality target is one bracket plus one linear solve — in rationals. Predictive parity is the same idea with a ratio of two linear functions, which is still one linear equation once you cross-multiply. That is why the repairs table can say “0.000000” and mean a BigInt zero.

It is also why precision has a hard ceiling: the most selective policy flags only the top bin, so the highest precision available in a group is the calibrated probability of that bin. When group A’s precision is above it, predictive parity is not expensive, it is impossible, and the page prints the bound that blocks it.

3. Enumeration instead of assertion

The impossibility is proved rather than described. For a pair of margins (n, PA) and (n, PB) the code walks every (tpA, fpA, tpB, fpB), tests the three equalities by integer cross-multiplication, and classifies every simultaneous solution by why it survived. Then it repeats over every pair of unequal margins, and then over every pair of equal margins as the control. The equal-margin control is the part that makes the zero informative: without it, a count of 0 could be a bug in the loop.

The relation itself is checked the same way, on every matrix, as an identity between two integer products — and its consequence is read back off the enumeration: among matrices with equal precision and equal recall, the ratio of false-positive rates equals the ratio of prevalence odds, for every one of them, as an exact rational equality.

4. The controls

Every headline here has a control measured on the same file, because a number with nothing beside it is not a finding. The group-blind payoff-optimal cut is the baseline every repair is scored against. The equal-base-rate enumeration is the control for the impossibility. The pooled and per-group calibration maps are the controls for shipping one map. The “move group A instead” column is the control for the direction of every repair. The t = 1 row is the control for the skill dial. And the total-variation bound is the control for unawareness: it says what blindness is worth before anyone claims it is worth everything.

5. Guards

Two things could quietly go wrong. The first is a broken identity hidden by a lucky configuration, so a sweep over 120 configurations — eight pairs of base rates, five skill levels, three bin counts — re-checks every identity in exact rationals and reports the count of non-zero residuals rather than a maximum, because in rationals the only interesting number is how many were not zero.

The second is the sampler. Day 69 reported an arithmetic seed stride that looked like a finding and was not, and this is the fifth re-measurement — the first against a target that is a closed form. Each seed family gets its own z against the exact gap, and the guard reports the mean z with its own standard error. A biased stream would show a mean z growing with the number of families; both streams sit inside one standard error, and again it does not reproduce.

One assertion in an earlier draft was relaxed rather than removed. A residual computed in floats is not exactly zero even when the rational is, because two summation orders differ at 1e-16, so anything float-valued on this page is asserted as a bound and anything claimed exact is asserted as r.n === 0n. The two are labelled differently in the identity table for that reason.

6. Verification

The block between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END is DOM-free, clock-free and free of the global random source; it is extracted by regex and run under Node against assertions written independently of it. The oracles share no code with the page: a forward simulator that builds each group’s confusion matrix by drawing which of the four cells every item fell into, against the page’s algebra; the score law recomputed as m independent Bernoulli draws rather than by inverting a CDF; the binomial normalisers (1+t)m and 2m as exact BigInt sums of C(m,v)tv, which is the binomial theorem checked rather than used; the total-variation distance recomputed as maxset |PA(set) − PB(set)| by enumerating all 2K subsets of the bins instead of by summing a positive part; the ROC and proxy AUCs by exhaustive pair counting; the Bayes cut by comparing every integer cut’s exact accuracy rather than by the closed form; and the impossibility by an independent enumeration in a different loop order with rational rather than cross-multiplied equality. Where the page uses a closed form the verifier recomputes it a second way; where the page enumerates, the verifier enumerates in a different order and compares the multiset of survivors.
