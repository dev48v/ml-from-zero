MachineLearningFromZero · Day 62

⚖️ Causal inference — every model in this series so far answered the same question, what value of y goes with this x, and every one of them would have been perfectly happy to be fed a column that causes nothing. Day 61's ARIMA forecast the next value, Day 60's survival model forecast a time, Day 1's regression forecast a number, and not one of them can tell you what would happen if you intervened, because none of them was ever asked. Today's question is different in kind, not degree: what would this outcome have been under the other treatment? That quantity — the potential outcome Y(1) against Y(0) — is not a prediction problem, because for every individual exactly one of the two is observed and the other is permanently missing. That is the fundamental problem of causal inference and it is not a data-collection failure you can fix with more rows. So "correlation is not causation" stops being a slogan and becomes an estimand problem: the thing you can compute, E[Y|T=1] − E[Y|T=0], and the thing you want, ATE = E[Y(1) − Y(0)], are two different numbers, and the gap between them is confounding — a variable X that pushes both who gets treated and what happens to them, so the two arms were never comparable to begin with. This page has a superpower the rest of the series does not: the data are generated from a known structural model where both potential outcomes are constructed for every unit, so the true ATE is known exactly, and every estimator is scored against ground truth live. You can watch the naive difference in means be confidently, reproducibly, reliably wrong — 3.37 when the truth is 2.00 — and then watch a single toggle, randomisation, sever the X→T arrow and pull it straight back to 2.01, which is the whole argument for the randomised trial in one slider. Then the repairs, all hand-rolled: the propensity score e(x) = P(T=1|X=x) from a real logistic regression fitted by IRLS, which Rosenbaum and Rubin proved is a balancing score — condition on that one number and X itself balances, demonstrated here live as a standardised mean difference falling from 0.98 to 0.12; matching, stratification, IPW in both its Horvitz–Thompson and Hájek forms with the effective sample size that exposes three units carrying the whole estimate, g-computation, and finally AIPW, which is correct if either of two models is right — a claim you can watch hold in three cells of a 2×2 and fail in the fourth. And because the assumption it all rests on is untestable, the page ends by breaking each assumption on purpose: hide a confounder and every method fails together, in agreement, silently; destroy overlap and the weights explode. The data below know the right answer. Watch the obvious estimator get it wrong. Every unit here is generated with both potential outcomes, Y(0) and Y(1), from the same noise draw — so the true average treatment effect is known to machine precision and there is a scoreboard. A confounder X drives both who gets treated and what happens to them, and the naive difference in means is off by exactly E[Y(0)|T=1] − E[Y(0)|T=0]. Randomisation fixes it by cutting one arrow. When you cannot randomise, the propensity score e(x) lets you rebuild the comparison four different ways — weight, match, stratify, or model the outcome — and AIPW lets you get two chances to be right. 🔨 Build it yourself

    
Causal inference is a logistic regression, a least-squares solve, a sort, and one estimating equation — about 250 lines of arithmetic with no dependencies. What makes it hard is not the code, it is knowing which number you are entitled to compute. Below is the exact runnable core the LOOK tab uses: a structural model that knows both potential outcomes, the naive estimator and its exact error, a hand-rolled IRLS logistic regression, standardised mean differences, nearest-neighbour matching with a caliper, stratification, Horvitz–Thompson and Hájek IPW with trimming and effective sample size, g-computation, AIPW, and a sensitivity analysis for the assumption you are never allowed to test.

    
    

      
  1. 
        
    1
    The estimand — decide what number you want before you compute anything
    
        
    Every unit has two potential outcomes: Y(1), what happens if treated, and Y(0), what happens if not. The individual effect is Y(1) − Y(0) and it is never observable, for anybody, ever — you see the one that corresponds to the treatment that actually happened and the other is missing by construction, not by accident. That is the fundamental problem of causal inference, and no sample size fixes it. What is recoverable is an average: the ATE E[Y(1) − Y(0)] over everyone, or the ATT E[Y(1) − Y(0) | T=1] over the treated only. Those are different numbers whenever the effect varies with who you are, and half the confusion in applied work is two people quoting two different estimands at each other. Write yours down first. The quantity you can compute from data alone, E[Y|T=1] − E[Y|T=0], is a third thing entirely, and it equals neither of them unless you have earned it.
    
        
    // what you WANT                       // what you CAN COMPUTE
ATE = E[ Y(1) - Y(0) ]                 assoc = E[Y | T=1] - E[Y | T=0]
ATT = E[ Y(1) - Y(0) | T = 1 ]

// they are equal only if assignment carries no information about the outcomes:
//     (Y(0), Y(1))  independent of  T          <-- "exchangeability"
// which a randomised trial BUYS and an observational study has to ARGUE FOR.

// consistency links the two worlds:  Y = T*Y(1) + (1-T)*Y(0)
// so E[Y | T=1] = E[Y(1) | T=1]   -- note the CONDITION did not go away.
//    E[Y(1)|T=1] = E[Y(1)] only under exchangeability. That step is the whole game.

    2
    A generator that knows the answer — so there is a scoreboard
    
        
    You cannot evaluate a causal estimator on real data, because the thing it estimates is unobservable — which is precisely why the field is full of methods that sound convincing and have never been scored. The way out is simulation from a known structural model: construct both potential outcomes yourself, reveal only one, and keep the other as the answer key. The one detail that makes this sharp is sharing the noise term between Y(0) and Y(1). If you draw independent noise for each arm the individual effect becomes random and the sample ATE wanders around τ; share it and the individual effect is exactly τ for every unit, so the sample ATE equals τ to machine precision and every disagreement you then see is real bias rather than Monte-Carlo fog.
    
        
    for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
  const x1 = gauss(rng), x2 = gauss(rng), eps = gauss(rng);

  const e  = sigmoid(a0 + a1*x1 + a2*x2);   // X drives WHO GETS TREATED
  const t  = rng() < e ? 1 : 0;

  const y0 = b0 + b1*x1 + b2*x2 + sig*eps;  // X drives WHAT HAPPENS
  const y1 = y0 + tau;                      // SAME eps -- effect is exactly tau

  Y[i] = t ? y1 : y0;                       // only ONE of them is revealed
}
// the answer key, computed from a column no estimator is allowed to touch:
sate     = mean(Y1[i] - Y0[i]);                            // === tau, exactly
confBias = mean(Y0 | T=1) - mean(Y0 | T=0);                // the naive error
// and therefore, identically:  naive === tau + confBias.  Verified over 60 seeds.

    3
    The naive estimator, and the exact size of its error
    
        
    Subtract the two group means and you have the estimator that ships in most dashboards. Decompose it and you can see precisely what it is doing wrong: it equals the truth plus E[Y(0)|T=1] − E[Y(0)|T=0], the difference in what would have happened to the two groups anyway, with no treatment involved. Confounding is that term and nothing else. It is not noise, so it does not shrink with n — on this page it sits at about +1.37 at a confounding strength of 1.0, and it sits there just as firmly at n = 3000 as at n = 200, which is the property that makes it dangerous: more data buys you a tighter confidence interval around the wrong number. When people say a result was "highly significant", this is the failure mode they have usually not ruled out.
    
        
    function naiveATE(T, Y) {
  let s1=0, n1=0, s0=0, n0=0;
  for (let i = 0; i < T.length; i++) { if (T[i]) { s1+=Y[i]; n1++; } else { s0+=Y[i]; n0++; } }
  return (n1 && n0) ? s1/n1 - s0/n0 : 0;
}
// E[Y|T=1] - E[Y|T=0]
//   = E[Y(1)|T=1] - E[Y(0)|T=0]                       (consistency)
//   = ATT + { E[Y(0)|T=1] - E[Y(0)|T=0] }             (add and subtract)
//     ^^^                ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
//     what you want      CONFOUNDING BIAS -- does NOT shrink with n
//
// measured on this page, n=4000, 60 seeds: naive 3.3701  truth 2.0000  bias 1.3701
// randomise the assignment and the SAME estimator returns 2.0078.

    The propensity score — a real logistic regression by IRLS
    
        
    The propensity score is e(x) = P(T=1 | X=x), the probability of being treated given what you know. Rosenbaum and Rubin's 1983 result is that it is a balancing score: if treatment is ignorable given X, it is also ignorable given the single scalar e(X), and X ⟂ T | e(X). That is a dimension-reduction theorem — however many covariates you had, you now have one number, and units with the same score are exchangeable in expectation. Fit it with maximum likelihood. There is no closed form, so use iteratively reweighted least squares: it is Newton's method on the log-likelihood, and each iteration is a weighted least-squares solve with weights p(1−p). Two guards matter in practice — a small ridge on the diagonal and a trust region on the step — because perfect separation genuinely happens and un-guarded Newton will happily send a coefficient to 1e100.
    
        
    // l(b) = sum [ y*log p + (1-y)*log(1-p) ],   p = sigmoid(x . b)
// dl/db = sum (y - p) x        d2l/db2 = -X' W X,   W = diag(p(1-p))
function logisticIRLS(X, y, lam) {
  let beta = zeros(p); beta[0] = logit(mean(y));
  for (let it = 0; it < 60; it++) {
    const XtWX = zeros(p,p), XtR = zeros(p);
    for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
      const pi = sigmoid(dot(X[i], beta));
      const w  = Math.max(pi*(1-pi), 1e-10);      // W collapses at the tails
      for (let a = 0; a < p; a++) {
        XtR[a] += (y[i]-pi) * X[i][a];
        for (let b = a; b < p; b++) XtWX[a][b] += X[i][a]*w*X[i][b];
      }
    }
    for (let a = 0; a < p; a++) { XtWX[a][a] += lam; XtR[a] -= lam*beta[a]; }
    const step = solveLin(XtWX, XtR);             // your own Gaussian elimination
    if (!step) break;
    const nrm = norm(step), sc = nrm > 4 ? 4/nrm : 1;   // trust region vs separation
    for (let a = 0; a < p; a++) beta[a] = clamp(beta[a] + sc*step[a], -30, 30);
    if (maxAbs(step)*sc < 1e-10) break;
  }
  return beta;
}
// TEST at the fitted beta:  dl/db  ===  lam * beta   (stationarity of the
// PENALISED objective).  Check the gradient itself by CENTRAL finite differences
// on UNTRAINED weights -- at convergence you are measuring round-off, not maths.

    Balance — the diagnostic that actually matters
    
        
    A propensity model is not judged by its AUC. A score that predicts treatment perfectly is a catastrophe, because it means the two arms do not overlap and there is nothing to compare. The only thing it is judged on is whether conditioning on it makes the covariates balanced, and the standard measure is the standardised mean difference — the gap between arm means in pooled standard deviations, which unlike a t-test does not get "better" simply because n went up. The convention is that |SMD| < 0.1 is acceptable, and it is a convention, not a theorem: it is a statement about a random quantity whose own standard deviation shrinks like 1/√n, so at n = 3000 it sits at about 0.049 and a single covariate will breach 0.1 by luck roughly once in sixty runs. Judge the whole love plot, and judge it after weighting, not before.
    
        
    function smd(x, T) {                               // unweighted
  const [a, b] = split(x, T);
  return (mean(a) - mean(b)) / Math.sqrt((variance(a) + variance(b)) / 2);
}
function smdWeighted(x, T, w) {                    // after IPW
  const m1 = wsum(w,x,T,1)/wsum(w,1,T,1), m0 = wsum(w,x,T,0)/wsum(w,1,T,0);
  // weighted variance with the effective-n correction  sum w - sum w^2 / sum w
  const v1 = wss(w,x,m1,T,1) / (sw1 - sq1/sw1), v0 = wss(w,x,m0,T,0) / (sw0 - sq0/sw0);
  return (m1 - m0) / Math.sqrt((v1 + v0) / 2);
}
// measured live on this page, X1 at confounding strength 1.2:
//   raw |SMD| 0.98   ->   after IPW 0.01   ->   after 1:1 caliper matching 0.15
// and the Rosenbaum-Rubin balancing-score property, checked directly:
//   marginal |SMD| 0.977   ->   mean WITHIN-STRATUM |SMD| 0.121  (8 strata of e(x))
// AUC is NOT a balance diagnostic. A perfect classifier means zero overlap.

    Matching and stratification — and what the discards do to your estimand
    
        
    Matching pairs each treated unit with the control nearest to it on the score — on the logit of the score, conventionally, because that stretches the compressed tails. A caliper refuses matches worse than some distance, usually 0.2 SD, which improves balance and quietly deletes the treated units that had no partner. That is the part people skip: the discarded units are exactly the ones with no comparable control, so the estimator that remains is not the ATT any more, it is the ATT among the matchable, and if 30% of the treated were dropped you should say so in the abstract. Stratification is the same idea with the sorting done once: split on quantiles of the score, take the within-stratum contrast, weight by stratum size. Cochran's old result is that five strata remove about 90% of the bias — on this page the residual after five strata is about 0.06 against an original bias of 1.37, which is 96%.
    
        
    function matchATE(T, Y, e, caliper, replacement) {
  const key = e.map(logit);                        // match on the LOGIT of e(x)
  const width = caliper * sd(key);                 // caliper in SDs, 0.2 is standard
  const ctrl = indicesWhere(T, 0).sort(byKey);     // sort once, binary-search after
  let sum = 0, k = 0, dropped = 0;
  for (const i of treatedSortedDescending) {
    const j = nearestAvailable(ctrl, key[i], width, replacement);
    if (j < 0) { dropped++; continue; }             // NO PARTNER -> unit is DELETED
    if (!replacement) markUsed(j);
    sum += Y[i] - Y[ctrl[j]]; k++;
  }
  return { ate: k ? sum/k : 0, nMatched: k, nDropped: dropped };
}
// with replacement the greedy answer IS the globally nearest one, so the test
// suite brute-forces every pair in O(nt*nc) and asserts an EXACT match.
// without replacement the result depends on the order you process treated units.
//
// matching targets the ATT.  With a heterogeneous effect they diverge and you can
// see it: measured here, ATE 1.997, ATT 2.590, NN matching 2.330, AIPW 1.996.

    IPW — reweight the sample into the trial you could not run
    
        
    Inverse-probability weighting takes the opposite route: keep everybody, but count each unit 1/e times if treated and 1/(1−e) times if not, so that a treated unit who was very unlikely to be treated stands in for all the people like them who were not. That builds a pseudo-population in which treatment is independent of X, and the plain difference in weighted means is unbiased for the ATE. The Horvitz–Thompson form divides by n and is unbiased but wild; the Hájek form divides by the sum of the weights instead, which is a self-normalising trick that trades a whisker of bias for a large drop in variance and is what you should almost always use. The diagnostic that matters is the Kish effective sample size (Σw)²/Σw². When 2000 rows have an effective sample size of 189, you do not have 2000 observations, you have 189, and no amount of confidence-interval arithmetic will conjure the rest back.
    
        
    const w = i => T[i] ? 1/e[i] : 1/(1 - e[i]);

// Horvitz-Thompson: unbiased, but the denominator is a constant n
ht    = (1/n) * sum( T[i]*Y[i]/e[i] - (1-T[i])*Y[i]/(1-e[i]) );

// Hajek / stabilised: self-normalising. slightly biased, far lower variance.
hajek = sum(T*Y/e)/sum(T/e) - sum((1-T)*Y/(1-e))/sum((1-T)/(1-e));

ess = (sum(w)**2) / sum(w**2);      // Kish. how many rows you REALLY have.

// POSITIVITY. e(x) near 0 or 1 makes 1/e enormous and the estimand undefined
// for those units. Clip to [eps, 1-eps] -- that is a real bias/variance trade,
// not a numerical convenience, and it must be reported:
e = clamp(e, eps, 1 - eps);        // hard floor 1e-6 so 1/e can never be Infinity
// measured live at overlap shift -2.5: min e(x) 2.7e-7, max weight 168,
// ESS 189 of 2000 rows, HT 1.52 vs Hajek 2.14 -- with a true ATE of 2.00.
// The gap between HT and Hajek IS the alarm. If they disagree, do not publish.

    g-computation — model the outcome, then predict a world that never happened
    
        
    The other half of the toolkit ignores the assignment mechanism entirely and models the outcome instead. Fit m₁(x) = E[Y | T=1, X=x] on the treated and m₀(x) = E[Y | T=0, X=x] on the controls, then push every unit through both and average the contrast. Note what that second step is: you are asking the treated-arm model to predict the controls, which is an extrapolation into a region it never saw, and the honesty of the whole exercise depends on those regions overlapping. Fitting two separate arm models rather than one model with a treatment dummy is worth the extra lines, because a single shared slope silently assumes the effect is the same for everybody. This is also the estimator that is most obviously a modelling choice — get the functional form wrong and it is wrong, with no compensating mechanism.
    
        
    function gComputation(cov, T, Y) {
  const f1 = ols(cov.filter(byArm(1)), Y.filter(byArm(1)));   // treated only
  const f0 = ols(cov.filter(byArm(0)), Y.filter(byArm(0)));   // controls only

  let s = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++)                     // EVERY unit, under BOTH arms
    s += dot(cov[i], f1) - dot(cov[i], f0);       // one of these is a counterfactual
  return s / n;
}
// this is the "g-formula": ATE = E_X[ E[Y|T=1,X] - E[Y|T=0,X] ], standardising
// over the covariate distribution of the WHOLE sample (restrict to the treated
// and you get the ATT instead).
// separate arm models, not one dummy: a shared slope ASSUMES a constant effect.
// the price: no overlap means you are extrapolating, and the model will not warn you.

    AIPW — two models, and you only need one of them to be right
    
        
    Doubly robust estimation combines the two: start from the g-computation contrast, then add an inverse-probability-weighted correction built out of the outcome model's own residuals. The algebra pays off twice. If the propensity model is right, E[T/e − 1 | X] = 0, so the correction terms average to zero no matter how badly the outcome model is misspecified, and you are left with an unbiased IPW-flavoured estimate. If the outcome model is right, the residuals average to zero conditional on X, so the correction vanishes no matter how badly the propensity model is misspecified, and you are left with g-computation. You get two shots. You do not get three — this page runs all four cells on identical data and the both-wrong cell is off by +0.44 while the other three land within 0.04 of the truth. There is also a deeper reason to prefer it: AIPW's error depends on the product of the two models' errors, so two merely decent models beat one excellent one.
    
        
    function aipwATE(T, Y, e, m1, m0) {
  let s = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    const corr = T[i] ? (Y[i] - m1[i]) / e[i]        // treated: reweight the residual
                      : -(Y[i] - m0[i]) / (1 - e[i]);
    s += m1[i] - m0[i] + corr;                       // g-computation + correction
  }
  return s / n;
}

    Sensitivity — the assumption you can never test, priced
    
        
    Everything above rests on conditional ignorability: given the covariates you measured, treatment is as good as random. Nothing in the data can check it. Hide one confounder on this page and every method — IPW, matching, stratification, g-computation, AIPW — moves together to about 0.82 against a truth of 2.00, in cheerful agreement, with balance tables that look immaculate on the covariates you did keep. Agreement between methods is not evidence; it only means they share an assumption. Since you cannot test it, price it: posit an unmeasured binary U that shifts the treatment logit by λ_T and the outcome by λ_Y, and compute how much bias that would leave. Then invert the question — how strong would U have to be to explain the entire result? If the answer is "about as strong as smoking is for lung cancer", your finding is robust. If it is "a mild nudge would do it", say so.
    
        
    // bias left behind = lamY * E_X[ P(U=1|T=1,X) - P(U=1|T=0,X) ]
function sensitivityBias(e, lamT, lamY, pU) {
  let s = 0;
  for (const e0 of e) {                             // e0 = the U=0 propensity
    const e1 = sigmoid(logit(e0) + lamT);           // ... and the U=1 propensity
    s +=   pU*e1     / (pU*e1     + (1-pU)*e0)
         - pU*(1-e1) / (pU*(1-e1) + (1-pU)*(1-e0));
  }
  return lamY * s / e.length;
}
// every term is a probability, so this cannot blow up. lam = 0 gives exactly 0.
// bisect on lam to find where the bias equals your estimate -- that is lam*.
//
// VERIFIED BY SIMULATION, not just derived: inject a real U of that strength,
// adjust for X only, measure what is left.  formula 0.1565 vs simulated 0.1645;
// formula 0.4198 vs simulated 0.4144; formula 0.3087 vs simulated 0.3053.
//
// in practice:

🎉 Day 62 of MachineLearningFromZero done — the first page in this series where "the model fits well" is not even relevant.
Causal inference is one substitution and a long argument about whether you are allowed to make it. Potential outcomes define the target: ATE = E[Y(1) − Y(0)], of which exactly one term per unit is ever observed. Confounding is the exact, computable gap E[Y(0)|T=1] − E[Y(0)|T=0] between what you can calculate and what you want — measured here at 1.3701, and it does not shrink with n. Randomisation deletes it by cutting the X→T arrow, which is why one toggle takes the same naive estimator from 3.3701 to 2.0078. When you cannot randomise, the propensity score e(x) = P(T=1|X) is a balancing score — one scalar that carries all the confounding, dropping the standardised mean difference from 0.977 to 0.121 within strata — and you may spend it four ways: match (and change the estimand to the ATT among the matchable), stratify, weight by 1/e with Hájek stabilisation and an effective sample size that tells you how many rows you really have, or ignore it and do g-computation on the outcome instead. AIPW refuses to choose and is right if either model is — 1.9985, 1.9654 and 1.9995 in three cells of the 2×2, and 2.4367 in the fourth. And then the part that separates this from statistics theatre: ignorability is untestable, so hide one confounder and watch every method agree on 0.82 with a flawless balance table, then price the assumption with a sensitivity analysis whose formula this page checks against a real simulation. 👉 Tomorrow — Day 63: continuing MachineLearningFromZero.
