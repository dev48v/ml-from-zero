STEP 1 OF 10

Two extremes, and neither one is right

When data arrives in groups — shops, schools, patients, A/B variants, servers — there are two obvious things to do and they are both wrong. Complete pooling throws the group labels away and gives every group the same global average; it is beautifully stable and it is blind to real differences that exist. No pooling gives every group its own average and nothing else; it respects every difference, including the ones that are pure noise. The first answer has no variance and plenty of bias; the second has no bias and an enormous amount of variance. The table below scores both against the truth on the seeded data, and the interesting column is the last one, because neither extreme is anywhere near it.

STEP 2 OF 10

A small group's average is mostly noise

The average of n observations, each carrying noise of variance σ², has variance σ²/n. That is the single most useful fact in this whole topic. With σ = 12, a shop with one rating has a sample mean whose standard deviation is 12 points — wider than the entire spread of true shop quality. A shop with 100 ratings has a standard deviation of 1.2 points. So the two numbers sitting next to each other in your leaderboard are not the same kind of number at all: one is a measurement, the other is very nearly a coin flip, and sorting them together treats them as equals.

STEP 3 OF 10

The groups are not strangers — they came from a population

Here is the modelling step everything else hangs on. Instead of treating the forty true scores as forty unrelated unknowns, treat them as forty draws from a population of shops with a mean μ and a spread τ. That single assumption is what makes the other 39 shops informative about this one: before you see any of shop #7's ratings you already know it is probably somewhere near μ, because every shop is. This is a prior, but an unusual one, because it is estimated from the data itself rather than asserted — which is why the method is called empirical Bayes. The observed spread of the group averages is bigger than τ, because it contains both real differences and sampling noise, and separating those two is step 5.

STEP 4 OF 10

The shrinkage weight is a ratio of two variances

Put the two previous steps together. You have a noisy measurement of shop g with variance σ²/ng, and a population that says the answer is probably near μ, with spread τ². The Gaussian posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the two, and it collapses into one line: λg = τ² / (τ² + σ²/ng), then estimate = λg · ŷg + (1 − λg) · μ. Read it as a signal-to-total-noise ratio: λ is the share of the observed spread that is real. It is bounded in [0, 1] automatically, it goes to 1 as n grows, and it goes to 0 as the group gets small or the population gets tight — so complete pooling and no pooling are not rivals of this method, they are its two endpoints.

λ for σ = 12, across group sizes and population spreads. Every entry is strictly between 0 and 1, strictly increasing left to right and strictly increasing top to bottom.

STEP 5 OF 10

You have to estimate both variances, and that is the hard part

σ² is easy: pool the within-group deviations and divide by N − G degrees of freedom — the one-rating shops contribute nothing to it, which is correct, because a single point has no spread. τ² is the hard one, because you never observe the true scores, only noisy versions of them. The trick is a method-of-moments identity: the weighted scatter of the group averages, Q, has an expected value of G − 1 when all the groups are truly identical, so any excess over G − 1 is attributable to real between-group spread. Subtract, rescale, and clamp at zero. That clamp is not decoration: a sample can genuinely look less spread out than chance alone would predict, and in that case the honest answer is τ̂² = 0, which collapses the method to complete pooling.

STEP 6 OF 10

What that does to one group, measured

Take shop #0 — genuinely above average — and vary nothing except how many ratings it has. The population parameters are re-estimated each time, so this is not an illusion produced by holding something fixed. With one rating the estimator keeps under a tenth of what the shop said about itself; with several hundred it keeps essentially all of it. Notice there is no threshold and no rule of thumb anywhere: the method does not decide a group is "too small", it continuously discounts it in proportion to how much of its number is noise. That is the difference between shrinkage and the usual fix of filtering out groups below some arbitrary minimum sample size.

STEP 7 OF 10

The scoreboard, over many seeded worlds

One world could be a fluke, so here is the same comparison repeated over independently seeded worlds: new true scores, new ratings, same estimators. Partial pooling is not winning by a nose. It also is not winning every time against no pooling, and that matters — the guarantee is about expected total error, not about every individual draw, and a page that claimed otherwise would be lying to you.

STEP 8 OF 10

Not a hack: this is the estimator that dominates

In 1961 James and Stein proved something that was genuinely unwelcome at the time. For a normal mean in three or more dimensions, the obvious estimator — use each group's own average, which is unbiased and is the maximum likelihood estimate — is inadmissible. A shrunken estimator has lower expected total squared error for every possible set of true means, with no exception, no prior to be right about and no regularity condition to check. That is a strictly stronger statement than "usually better". The version below is the classical one with equal group sizes and a shrink factor c = 1 − (G−3)σ²/n / Σ(ŷ−μ̂)², and the table below scores it against the obvious estimator on sixty independently seeded worlds.

STEP 9 OF 10

Error moved out of variance and into bias, on purpose

This is the part that makes people uncomfortable, so it is worth measuring rather than asserting. Fix the forty true scores once, then redraw only the ratings, many times. For the one-rating shop, the no-pooling estimate is almost perfectly unbiased and almost perfectly useless: its error is nearly all variance. Partial pooling introduces a real, visible, systematic bias — it pulls that shop toward the middle every single time, and the bias does not average away — and in exchange the variance collapses by two orders of magnitude. The total is smaller. Being unbiased was never the objective; being close was.

STEP 10 OF 10

The leaderboard lesson, and when this hurts

Rank the shops by their raw averages and pick the winner. Now rank them by the shrunken estimates and pick again. Both lists are built from the same data, and one of them keeps nominating shops with two ratings. The raw top five is dominated by tiny groups not because tiny groups are good, but because tiny groups have the widest distribution of outcomes and a maximum picks from the tails.

And the honest caveat

Partial pooling buys lower total error by accepting higher error on some individual groups. A group that is genuinely extreme gets dragged toward the middle, and if your decision is about that one group specifically — a safety alert, a fraud case, a single hospital's mortality rate — the shrunken number is the wrong one to argue from. The pooling assumption also has to be true: it says the groups are exchangeable draws from one population, and if they are not, shrinking toward a mean that no group belongs to makes things worse.
