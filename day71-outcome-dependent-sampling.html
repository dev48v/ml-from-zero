🎯 Selection on the outcome — days 68 to 70 asked what a column was hiding. This one asks why the row is in the file at all. Let s₁ = P(recorded | y=1) and s₀ = P(recorded | y=0); condition on being recorded and the odds simply multiply, so logit q = logit η + log κ with κ = s₁/s₀ — a constant shift, identical in every cell. Then the part that breaks the arc: Day 69 and Day 70 got their bounds out of “this has to be a probability”, and here that constraint is vacuous — expit lands in (0,1) whatever κ is, so a sweep of 20,001 worlds across twelve orders of magnitude rejects 0. Nothing in the dataset bounds it. The bound has to come from the design, and it does: s ≤ 1 gives fπ' ≤ π ≤ 1 − f(1−π'), an interval of width exactly 1 − f — one minus the sampling fraction, not the sample size, and independent of the recorded base rate to 0e+0. So one file, row for row, supports a prevalence of 0.0250 or 0.9750; sensitivity, specificity, both likelihood ratios and ROC-AUC come back identical to 1.3e-15 across every world while precision reads 0.6896 and is truly 0.1000; the fitted slope is 2.2000 everywhere and the intercept moves by exactly log κ to 1.3e-15; and the same 95% interval covers precision 94.95% of the time in one world and 0.00% in the other four — a figure computed by summing binomial probabilities, not simulated. Calibration is perfect on the sample (ECE exactly 0) and off by 0.3067 in truth. You type a threshold of 0.20 and the machine runs 0.0123. Accuracy inverts 9,930 of 32,640 model pairs; balanced accuracy inverts 0, structurally. And selecting on a continuous y attenuates the slope to 0.4102 while selecting on x at the same standardised cut leaves it at 1.0000 — same retention rate, same R², and the sample with the narrower range is the unbiased one. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

The feature is discrete — K cells, each with a mass and a recorded positive rate — so every population quantity here is a finite sum and the ground truth is arithmetic rather than simulation. The observed law is fixed: the sampler is handed (mS, q) and there is no third argument. A world is one number, κ = s₁/s₀, which forces ηk = expit(logit qk − log κ) and mk ∝ mS,k / (κηk + 1 − ηk). The recorded base rate is 0.500; the design bound puts the true prevalence in [0.025, 0.975], width 0.950. Coverage is over 1000 replications and everything else is averaged over 25 seeds unless the row says otherwise.

in-page checks

Why is this row in the file?

Three days of this series have been about what a column is hiding. Day 68 asked what the blank cells were doing, Day 69 treated a predictor as a recording of something else, Day 70 did the same to the target. Each time the question was: given that this row exists, how much of what it says can I trust?

This is the level above. A row is in your file because something put it there — a claim was filed, a test was ordered, a customer complained, a case was reviewed, a patient presented. If the thing that put it there also depends on the outcome you are trying to predict, then your sample is not a shrunken copy of the population. It is a different population, and it differs in exactly one way that is worth knowing precisely.

Write s₁ = P(recorded | y=1) and s₀ = P(recorded | y=0). Selection is allowed to look at y and nothing else — not at x, not at anything unobserved. That is a real restriction and it is what makes the whole page exact. Condition on being recorded, in a cell where the true positive rate is η:

P(y=1 | cell, recorded)     m η s₁             q            η
------------------------ = ---------------  =>  ----- = κ -------
P(y=0 | cell, recorded)     m (1-η) s₀         1-q          1-η

                logit q = logit η + log κ,    κ = s₁/s₀

The cell mass cancels. The result does not depend on which cell you are in. Selection on the outcome adds one constant to every log-odds and does nothing else at all — and the rest of this page is what falls out of that single sentence.

The bound that is not there

Day 69 and Day 70 both got a real bound out of a very cheap fact. Invert the observed law to recover the truth, insist that the recovered quantity is a legal probability or a legal variance, and the inequality writes itself. Day 69 got λ ≥ R², the Frisch bound. Day 70 got ρ₀ ≤ min q and ρ₁ ≤ 1 − max q.

Run the move here and it produces nothing. The inverse is η = expit(logit q − log κ), and expit maps the entire real line into (0,1). Every κ in (0, ∞) gives legal probabilities. The population masses, m ∝ mS/(κη + 1 − η), are positive for every κ too. The page sweeps twelve orders of magnitude and rejects nothing, because there is nothing to reject.

This is worth sitting with, because it is a sharper failure than the two before it. On Day 69 a strong fit bought you a tight identified set. On Day 70 a confident model bought you one. Here no property of the data buys you anything. The recorded base rate is compatible with every prevalence in (0,1), and no amount of extra rows, extra features or better separation changes that by a hair.

One minus the sampling fraction

What does bound it is a fact about the study rather than about the data. If the sample is a fraction f = n/N of the population, then the two selection probabilities are pinned to the prevalence:

s₁ = fπ'/π         s₀ = f(1-π')/(1-π)

s₁ ≤ 1   =>   π ≥ fπ'
s₀ ≤ 1   =>   π ≤ 1 - f(1-π')

width = 1 - fπ' - f(1-π') = 1 - f

The recorded base rate cancels out of the width completely. A hundred thousand rows drawn from a hundred million pin the prevalence to within 0.999 — the same interval you would get from a hundred rows out of a hundred thousand. It is not the sample size that identifies anything, it is the fraction. This is why a case-control study reports odds ratios and stops: the design deliberately throws away the only quantity that could have told you the prevalence, and it does so on purpose, because that is what buys the precision.

The half of your report that is still true

Selection on the outcome does something very specific to the joint distribution. Positives are kept with probability s₁ whatever their features, negatives with s₀ whatever theirs. So within a class, the mix of features is untouched:

p(x | y=1, recorded) = p(x | y=1)        p(x | y=0, recorded) = p(x | y=0)

Everything built only out of those two conditionals is therefore exactly right on a selected sample. Sensitivity. Specificity. Both likelihood ratios. The whole ROC curve and its area. The slope of a logistic fit — because logit q = (β₀ + log κ) + β₁x and the shift lands entirely on the intercept. That last one is Prentice and Pyke's result, and it is the reason case-control studies work at all.

Everything that mixes the two classes together is wrong, and wrong by a factor you cannot estimate: prevalence, precision, negative predictive value, F₁, lift, expected cost, calibration, and accuracy. The page prints both blocks side by side. The invariant block agrees to the last bit; the other block is off by up to 0.62.

Calibration cannot see it

The natural instinct is that a calibration plot would catch this. It cannot, and the reason is structural rather than statistical: the model was fitted to the labels the sample has. Its predicted probabilities match the observed frequencies because that is what fitting means. Expected calibration error on the sample is not small, it is exactly zero.

Against the population the error is q − η in every cell, all of one sign, and the largest it can ever be has a closed form. Maximise expit(u + a) − expit(u) over u: the derivative σ'(u+a) = σ'(u) forces u = −a/2 by symmetry, and the value there is σ(a/2) − σ(−a/2) = tanh(a/4). So a twentyfold oversampling of cases can push a single probability out by tanh(log 20 / 4) = 0.6345, and the page finds that supremum twice — once from the formula and once from a dense scan.

The threshold in force

The practical consequence is not that the probabilities are decorative. It is that they are still used. Set a threshold t on the recorded probability and you have set a different threshold on the truth:

q ≥ t   <=>   η ≥ expit(logit t - log κ)

At the default settings you type 0.20 and the machine runs 0.0123. That is not a mild mis-tuning, it is a sixteenfold change in the odds you are willing to act on, and it survives every review, because the number in the config file says 0.20. The cost is measured rather than described: the naive rule spends 3.2 times the optimal cost, and it is not a fragile configuration — the rules disagree on five of eight cells.

What to actually do

None of this argues against outcome-dependent sampling. The last table on the MEASURE tab is the case for it: at a prevalence of 0.025 a balanced case-control draw estimates the slope three times more precisely than a random draw of the same size, and the reason is simply that a random draw of two thousand rows contains fifty positives. Deliberate selection on the outcome is often the best design available.

What follows is narrower, and worth stating as rules:

  • Report sensitivity, specificity and AUC. They are exactly right and they need no assumptions at all.
  • Never report precision, F₁, accuracy or a calibration curve from a selected sample without an external prevalence. They are not noisy, they are answering a different question.
  • Rank models on balanced accuracy, not accuracy. The page shows the difference is not academic: 9,930 of 32,640 pairs swap order under accuracy and zero swap under balanced accuracy.
  • The correction is one subtraction, logit p − log κ, and it is exact once you know the prevalence. An external estimate from four hundred rows already beats the uncorrected number by 29 times.
  • Write down the sampling fraction. It is the only quantity that turns the prevalence from unidentified into bounded, and it is usually knowable and rarely recorded.

🔨 How it is built

1. A discrete feature, so the truth is a sum

The feature is K cells, each with a mass and a recorded positive rate. That single choice removes every integral from the binary half of the page: prevalence, precision, Bayes accuracy, expected cost and AUC are all finite sums, computed in exact arithmetic rather than estimated from a held-out split. Nothing on the MEASURE tab is scored against a simulation except the things that are explicitly Monte Carlo, and those are labelled.

2. The sampler is not given a world

sampleObserved(law, n, seed) takes three arguments and none of them is κ. There is nowhere to put one. That is the structural version of the claim, and the page still counts it rather than resting on it: eight seeds, four world pairs, 1,500 rows each, comparing the cell index and the label of every row. 48,000 comparisons, zero differences, and twelve derived diagnostics compared per pair for good measure.

3. A second, harder identity

The stronger statement is the one about balanced draws. Because p(x|y) is invariant, a case-control sample taken from world A and one taken from world B are not merely similar — they are the same rows, drawn from class-conditional distributions that agree to 2.2e-16. The page draws them from five genuinely different populations and compares the fitted coefficients: identical, to zero. Two different worlds, two different prevalences, one dataset.

4. Where === is right and where it is not

Row indices and labels are integers, so the identity audit uses !== and expects an exact zero. Algebra does not get that treatment. Day 68 shipped a bit-equality assertion on a true algebraic identity and it failed at 1.6e-15 purely on summation order, so every identity here is checked with a tolerance and the tolerance is chosen against what the arithmetic can deliver: 1e-12 for sums over cells, 1e-6 where the normal CDF is involved, because the tail approximation in use is good to 1.2e-7 and no assertion should pretend otherwise.

The model-selection counts needed the same care in reverse. Two rules whose balanced accuracies are equal can still come out with opposite signs at 1e-16, and counting those as inversions produced 22 phantom ones. The fix is a tie tolerance — and the number of pairs it skips is printed, because a silent tolerance is exactly how a structural zero gets manufactured.

5. The whole hypothesis space, not a sample of it

Model selection enumerates every rule in {0,1}K — 256 of them at the default K, and all 32,640 pairs — rather than fitting a handful of models and hoping they are representative. The zero for balanced accuracy is then a statement about the whole space, and the 9,930 for accuracy is a count rather than an estimate. The admissible worlds get the same treatment: 20,001 values of κ across twelve orders of magnitude, every cell checked, rather than an argument that the constraint is vacuous.

6. Two routes to every closed form

The calibration supremum tanh(a/4) is derived and then found again by a 40,000-point scan. The matched x-truncation cut is derived algebraically and then found again by bisection on the numerical R², agreeing to 4.4e-16. The Mills ratio is computed from the complementary error function and then again from its continued fraction, agreeing to 1.0e-7. When a number on this page has a formula, the formula is confirmed by something that does not use it.

7. A tail approximation that is fit for a tail

The usual Abramowitz & Stegun 7.1.26 rational bounds its absolute error at 1.5e-7, which is worthless when the quantity you want is a tail worth 1e-30 — and the Mills ratio is a ratio of two tail quantities. The first version of the truncation module used it and produced δ > 1, hence a negative attenuation factor, at cuts past about three standard deviations. Numerical Recipes' erfcc bounds the fractional error at 1.2e-7 instead, and with it the two exponentials in φ(α)/(1−Φ(α)) cancel exactly. An 8,000-point sweep of (b, σ, cut) now finds zero violations of δ < 1.

8. Coverage against an exact number, not a bigger simulation

The interval on the MEASURE tab is a Wald interval for precision, and conditional on landing in the predicted-positive set a row is Bernoulli with a fixed probability, so the count of true positives given the denominator is exactly Binomial. That means the coverage has a closed form: sum the binomial pmf over both the denominator and the numerator. It comes to 94.950%.

This mattered. At 1,000 replications the simulation read 96.0%, which looks like a finding about Wald intervals. At 2,000 it read 95.75%, at 4,000 95.88%. Against the exact 94.950% and a standard error of 0.35 points that is a two-and-a-half sigma discrepancy, so the page ran 100,000 replications with three different seed streams. Sequential seeds gave 94.951%. The multiplicative hash gave 95.041%. There was no artefact — the first 20,000 draws of that particular hash stream were simply an unlucky stretch, and the whole thing was Monte Carlo noise wearing a suit. The exact column is on the page permanently so the question cannot come back.

9. The seed guard, run against a known answer

Day 69 measured a 99.2% coverage that turned out to be an arithmetic seed stride feeding Box–Muller; Day 70 re-measured it for a uniform-only sampler and found it did not reproduce. This page has both kinds of sampler, so it runs both streams through both, and it can do something neither previous day could: compare them to an exact target rather than to each other. Neither stream is distinguishable from the truth at the replication counts used, and the numbers are printed rather than summarised, including the standard error, so a reader can see how much the comparison is worth.

10. Verification

The block between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END is DOM-free and is extracted by regex and run under Node against assertions written independently of it: a different generator (xorshift32 with the sine branch of Box–Muller against the page's mulberry32 and cosine), a normal CDF by Simpson integration of the density against the page's rational tail, binomial probabilities by a multiplicative recurrence against the page's log-gamma, and — the one that matters — a forward selection simulator that draws a row from the population and then accepts or rejects it with probability s₁ or s₀. The page never does that. It never draws a population row at all; it starts from the recorded law. If the odds-multiplication argument were wrong, the accepted sample from the forward simulator would not match the recorded law, and that is the check.

11. Things I got wrong

Three, and the first one nearly shipped as a headline. The default configuration was a symmetric observed law read at a threshold of 0.5, and in that configuration accuracy came back invariant — sample and population agreeing to 1.1e-16, sitting in a table of quantities that are genuinely invariant. It is not invariant. Accuracy is spec + π(sens − spec), and at that exact threshold the symmetric law makes sensitivity equal specificity, so the prevalence weight has nothing to weight. Moving the threshold one notch breaks it. The default threshold is now 0.35, and the coincidence has its own table on the MEASURE tab, because a knife-edge that looks like a law is worth more than the law would have been.

Second: the AUC was computed the wrong way round and read 0.176 for a model that separates well. It was still perfectly invariant across worlds, which is the uncomfortable part — an invariance check passes just as happily on a broken statistic. Third: the negative attenuation factor described in section 7, which the sweep caught rather than the eye.

12. What this is not

Selection here is allowed to depend on y and only on y. That is what makes p(x|y) invariant and everything else exact. If selection also depends on x — if reviewers looked at the features before deciding what to file — then the class-conditional distributions move too, sensitivity and AUC stop being identified, and none of the reassuring half of this page survives. The continuous module is the honest illustration: truncating on y and truncating on x are genuinely different mechanisms, and the page shows they can produce the same retention rate and the same R² while one leaves the slope alone and the other does not.
