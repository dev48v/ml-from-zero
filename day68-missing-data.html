MACHINELEARNINGFROMZERO · DAY 68

🫥 Missing data — the rows that are not there decide the answer. Three mechanisms get named: MCAR (missing for no reason), MAR (missing for a reason you recorded) and MNAR (missing because of the value itself). The standard advice is to test which one you have, and the standard test compares the variables you do observe across the missingness pattern. This page builds the whole mechanism space out of two numbers — β for x-dependence, δ for y-dependence — so the truth is exact, then hands two different worlds the same observed dataset: same x array, same missingness mask, same recorded y values, cell for cell. Every diagnostic, every imputation, every confidence interval is bit-identical across the pair, and the quantity being estimated is not. Multiple imputation's 95% interval covers the truth 93.6% of the time in one world and 0.0% in the other — the same interval, both times. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, offline.

Truth: x ~ N(0,1), y = 1 + b·x + ε, so E[y], Var(y), corr(x,y) and the slope are all known exactly. Missingness is P(observed | x) = Φ(α + βx) with α solved so the observed fraction is exactly r whatever β is, and the missing y's are drawn δ above the observed ones at the same x. β = 0 and δ = 0 is MCAR; δ = 0 is MAR; δ ≠ 0 is MNAR. Every number below is averaged over 25 seeds.

IN-PAGE SELF-TEST

Three mechanisms, and only two of them are testable

Every dataset with holes in it has a second, invisible dataset attached: the mechanism that decided which cells you got. Write R for the indicator "this value was recorded". The three names are three statements about what R depends on.

  • MCAR. P(R | x, y) = P(R). The holes are unrelated to anything. Dropping them costs you sample size and nothing else.
  • MAR. P(R | x, y) = P(R | x). The holes depend only on things you have. Sick patients skip the follow-up visit — and you recorded that they were sick.
  • MNAR. P(R | x, y) genuinely depends on y. High earners decline to state their income; the reason the value is missing is the value.

MCAR is a special case of MAR, not an alternative to it — which is why a test that "rejects MCAR" has told you almost nothing about whether your method is safe.

The whole space is two numbers

On this page the mechanism is P(R = 1 | x) = Φ(α + βx), and the missing y's are drawn δ above the recorded ones at the same x. That is the entire generator:

β = 0, δ = 0   →  MCAR
β ≠ 0, δ = 0   →  MAR
δ ≠ 0          →  MNAR   (whatever β is)

δ is not a hand-wave. Fixing the recorded conditional at N(μ(x), τ²) and the missing one at N(μ(x) + δ, τ²) implies a missingness model, and Bayes gives it in closed form:

logit P(R = 1 | x, y) = logit P(R = 1 | x) + δ²/2τ² − (δ/τ²)(y − μ(x))

The coefficient on y is exactly −δ/τ². At δ = 0 it is exactly zero and the mechanism does not depend on y at all — which is the definition of MAR, arrived at rather than asserted. The page checks that identity numerically instead of trusting the algebra.

Why the twin worlds are the whole point

Both worlds are built from the same call to the sampler that produces the observed data — a function that is not given δ. World A fills the holes with δ = 0, world B with δ > 0. You are handed x, the mask, and the recorded y's, and those are the same arrays.

So any function of the observed data returns the same value in both worlds. Not approximately: bit for bit. The t-test, Little's MCAR test, a logistic regression of the mask on every column you have, the complete-case estimate, mean imputation, multiple imputation, its standard error, its degrees of freedom, its confidence interval. And the quantity all of that is estimating differs by δ(1 − r). The distinction between MAR and MNAR is not hard to test. It is not a function of the data.

That is a much stronger statement than the usual "MNAR is hard". Hard problems have better estimators. This one has none, because two different answers are consistent with the identical evidence — and only an assumption, from outside the data, picks between them.

What the standard test is actually testing

Comparing the observed variables across the missingness pattern — Little's test in its one-variable form — is a test of β, and only of β. On the MEASURE tab it has full power on the axis that barely matters and, by construction, none at all on the axis that decides the answer. Both of the following are on the page:

  • A pair where the test screams in both worlds (identical t), one of which multiple imputation handles perfectly and one of which it does not handle at all.
  • A pair where the test sits at its 5% noise floor in both worlds — the dataset that looks textbook-MCAR — and one of them has a complete-case estimate off by δ(1 − r).

A quiet diagnostic is evidence about β. It is not evidence about δ.

Complete-case: it depends what you are estimating

"Listwise deletion is biased under MAR" is half a sentence. Selecting rows on x changes the distribution of x, so it changes E[y]. It does not change p(y | x) — so a regression of y on x, fitted on complete cases only, is unbiased for the slope under exactly the MAR mechanism that ruins the mean. The page measures both against exact values.

What breaks the slope is selection on y. The fifth world truncates: any y above a threshold is never recorded. Now the recorded conditional is a truncated normal, the slope attenuates, and the closed form for how much is on the page next to the measurement.

Imputation: four ways to be right and one way to be honest

Filling holes in is not one method, and the methods fail in different columns:

  • Mean imputation gives you back the complete-case mean — the same number, as an algebraic identity — while quietly shrinking the variance to r · s² and pulling the correlation towards zero. (The page asserted that identity bit for bit and the assertion failed: the two routines add up the same quantity in different orders, so they land 1.6e-15 apart. Fifteen significant figures of agreement, and not sixteen.)
  • Regression imputation fixes the mean and puts every imputed point exactly on the fitted line, so the correlation comes back too strong. Two methods, two opposite directions of error on the same column.
  • Stochastic regression imputation adds the residual noise back and gets all four point estimates right — and still reports a standard error computed as if the imputed values were data.
  • Multiple imputation is the only one of the five that admits it guessed: m completed datasets, pooled by Rubin's rules, with a between-imputation variance term that exists precisely to price the guess.

Mean imputation's interval failure is arithmetic, not bad luck. The reported standard error is simp/√n and the real one is sobs/√(rn), so the interval is too narrow by a factor of exactly r and the coverage lands on 2Φ(1.96r) − 1. At r = 0.6 that is 76.0%, and the page measures 77.6% against the prediction. A nominal 95% interval that covers three quarters of the time is not a rounding error, and nothing about the point estimate looks wrong.

Multiple imputation is not free of this either, and the page does not round it up: at m = 10 its MAR coverage measures 93.6% against a nominal 95%, a residual undercoverage from the approximate Bayesian bootstrap at a small number of imputations. That is a one-point problem. The MNAR column below it is a ninety-four-point problem.

The honest caveat about this page

One covariate, Gaussian everything, a correctly specified linear model, and imputation models that match the truth exactly. That is deliberate: it removes every other source of error, so what is left is the mechanism. Real imputation also has to get the model right, and that only adds problems. The structural claims — the observed data being identical across the twins, the implied coefficient on y being exactly −δ/τ², mean imputation returning the complete-case mean bit for bit, the variance identity — do not depend on any of it. The specific magnitudes do.

🔨 How it is built

1. A sampler that cannot see δ

sampleBase(n, cfg, beta, seed)   // -> { x, R, yObs, yMisBase }   NO delta argument
completeY(base, delta)           // -> y, differing from base ONLY on the missing cells

The twin claim is enforced by the function signature. Two worlds with the same (beta, seed) are handed the same cached base object, so "the observed data is identical" is not something the page checks afterwards and hopes for — it is the only thing that could have happened. The complete vectors then differ by exactly δ on exactly the missing cells, which the self-test verifies both ways.

2. The observed fraction is exactly r for every β

c = Φ⁻¹(r);   α = c·√(1 + β²)
E[Φ(α + βx)] = Φ(α/√(1 + β²)) = Φ(c) = r

Without that solve, turning up β would also change how much data you have, and every comparison across the table would confound the two. With it, β moves the shape of the missingness and nothing else. Φ⁻¹ is a bisection on the page's own Φ, so the two are consistent to machine precision even though Φ itself is only good to 1.5e-7.

3. Closed forms for everything the table prints

E[y]            = a + δ(1 − r)
E[x | R = 1]    = (β/√(1+β²)) · φ(c)/r          // and E[x|R=0] = −(…)/(1−r)
CC mean target  = a + b·E[x | R = 1]
CC slope target = b                              // p(y|x) is untouched by selection on x
truncated world : E[y | y < t] = a − σᵢ·φ(c)/Φ(c)

The decomposition r·E[x|R=1] + (1−r)·E[x|R=0] = 0 is asserted to 1e-12 before any of it is used, because that identity is the one that fails first if the selection algebra is wrong. The truncated world's attenuated slope has no tidy closed form, so it is integrated: the inverse-Mills conditional mean μ(x) − τφ(z)/Φ(z) is fed through a Simpson quadrature of the population least-squares slope, and the quadrature is checked by making it reproduce the mean, which does have a closed form.

4. The implied MNAR mechanism, derived not declared

P(R=1 | x,y) = p·f₀(y) / (p·f₀(y) + (1−p)·f₁(y))     // Bayes, computed always
⇒ logit P(R=1|x,y) = logit p + δ²/2τ² − (δ/τ²)(y − μ(x))

The page never branches on δ to decide whether the mechanism depends on y — it computes the posterior every time and reads the slope off numerically with a central difference. At δ = 0 that slope comes back 0 and the whole y-grid returns the same probability, which is MAR falling out of the arithmetic rather than being stipulated.

5. Five estimators behind one interface

completeCase / meanImpute / regressionImpute / stochasticImpute / multipleImpute
   -> { mean, sd, corr, slope, se, lo, hi }

Every method produces a completed y (or a subset) and the same statsOf reads all four estimands off it, so no method gets a bespoke code path that could flatter it. Multiple imputation draws its parameters from an approximate Bayesian bootstrap of the recorded rows — resample, refit, impute with residual noise — then pools with Rubin's rules, including the (1 + 1/m)B term and the degrees of freedom that follow from it. Its t quantile is a Cornish–Fisher expansion, checked in the verifier against a numerically integrated t distribution.

6. Two identities that catch a lot

meanImpute.mean ≡ completeCase.mean                   // algebraically; 1.6e-15 in floats
s²imp ≡ Σobs(y − ŷobs)² / (n − 1)                     // to 1e-12

The first is why mean imputation is so persistent: it changes no number anyone looks at. The second is why it is so damaging: the imputed cells contribute exactly zero to the sum of squares, so the variance is deflated by exactly the response rate and every interval built from it is too narrow by exactly r.

The first was written as === and it failed. Complete-case adds the recorded y's and divides by k; mean imputation adds the recorded y's plus (n−k) copies of their average and divides by n. Same number on paper, two different summation orders in binary64, 1.6e-15 apart. The test now asserts the identity to 1e-12 and says why — a === that has to be relaxed is worth keeping visible rather than quietly loosening.

7. What the tests pin

assert(P(R|x) is constant across x) when β = 0;        // MCAR is a value, not a mode
assert(P(R|x,y) does not move with y) when δ = 0;      // MAR, derived from Bayes
assert(implied ∂logit/∂y === −δ/τ²);
assert(worldA.base === worldB.base);                    // same object, not just equal
assert(yB − yA === δ on missing, === 0 on observed);
assert(diagnostic(A) === diagnostic(B));                // to the last bit
assert(MI(A) === MI(B)) while truth(A) ≠ truth(B);
assert(meanImpute.mean === completeCase.mean);
assert(coverage(meanImpute | MCAR) ≈ 2Φ(1.96r) − 1);
